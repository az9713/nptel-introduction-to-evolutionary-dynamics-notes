Long-term evolution and fitness trajectories

Twelve populations repeatedly enter the same fresh medium. Their descendants increasingly outperform the ancestor, but the improvements become smaller. An investigator must decide whether the populations are approaching a final optimum, whether they still have useful mutations available, and whether a changing mutation rate is itself an adaptation. A smooth rising curve answers none of these questions by itself. The experiment needs a recoverable ancestor, a defined fitness assay, competing mathematical descriptions, and evidence about the mutations that produced the curve.

Environmental change and adaptation have competing time scales Lecture 50, 00:01

The spatial antibiotic experiment from the preceding chapter permits growth in intermediate environments before descendants encounter stronger challenges. This supplies more reproductions and therefore more opportunities for relevant variants to arise. A sudden challenge can remove that opportunity if the population cannot reproduce in the new condition. Yet an uncrossed barrier in a finite experiment establishes a failure under that particular protocol, not the genetic impossibility of surviving every abrupt environmental change.

The same distinction matters for environmental deterioration. The rate at which conditions change competes with the times needed for reproduction, mutation, establishment and population expansion. Large standing populations may already contain useful variants, while a severely reduced population can have little further mutation supply. Tolerance depends on the starting genotypes and physiological state as well as on the rate of environmental change. Neither a universal maximum tolerable rate nor an ecological extinction percentage follows from the MEGA-plate demonstration.

Bacterial populations can also acquire existing DNA by horizontal gene transfer, meaning transfer between lineages rather than ordinary parent-to-offspring inheritance. Transformation takes up environmental DNA; conjugation transfers DNA through contact; bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, can move bacterial DNA between hosts. These routes differ from a new mutation within the recipient's genome. A particular experiment can document adaptation by mutations without showing that horizontal transfer is unimportant in every natural population. The investigator must specify which genetic routes the apparatus permits and which were actually measured.

Dallinger's experiment demonstrates tolerance change, with historical limits Lecture 50, 13:18

Before turning to the modern twelve-population experiment, the lecture describes a nineteenth-century attempt to observe adaptation over many generations. Dallinger slowly increased the temperature surrounding microscopic organisms and interrupted the increases when their activity declined. The original report is necessary here because the spoken bacterial narrative and numerical growth comparison do not preserve the historical measurements.

In his 1887 President's Address, pp. 190–199, Dallinger followed three specified microscopic forms he called monads, rather than a defined bacterial strain. He began at 60°F, about 15.6°C, and eventually reached 158°F, or 70°C. His account describes long pauses, impaired activity and recovery, and temperature-dependent changes in visible cell structure. The terminal apparatus accident prevented completion of the intended experiment. It was a demanding observation program, but it was not a modern replicated genomic study.

The report also describes a striking environmental reversal: the adapted organisms placed back at 60°F died, while organisms maintained at low temperature failed under sufficiently high temperature. This supports his reported change in tolerance and motivates the idea of a trade-off between environments. It does not supply the lecture's schematic bacterial growth-rate comparison at 25°C. Nor do these observations alone distinguish DNA-sequence evolution from physiological acclimation, altered community composition, or other persistent changes. Dallinger himself limited the inference to the forms observed.

The running investigator can learn two lessons. A gradual challenge makes survival and reproduction during the transition possible, but that is not sufficient evidence for a particular molecular mechanism. To establish inherited genetic adaptation now, one would isolate descendants, grow ancestor and descendant under matched common conditions, assay their responses across temperatures, identify changes in their genomes and reconstruct candidate variants. An environmental response measured immediately after exposure can include physiology; a difference that persists after controlled passage more strongly supports inheritance, although identifying the causal changes still requires additional work.

A recoverable ancestor turns a culture history into a comparative experiment Lecture 50, 18:27 Lecture 51, 00:01

The long-term evolution experiment, abbreviated LTEE, began in 1988 with twelve populations of Escherichia coli. Its defining structure is repeated growth and transfer from a common ancestral background, with replicate populations maintained separately. Six populations initially carried one state of an arabinose-use marker and six the other. The marker allows competitors to be distinguished on appropriate assay plates; its use as a neutral marker is conditional on the experimental environment and must be checked rather than assumed for every medium.

The historical protocol described by Lenski and Travisano (1994), Methods, p. 6809, used daily 1:100 transfers into 10 mL of glucose-limited minimal medium at 37°C with aeration. Cells experience growth, resource depletion and time without further substantial expansion before the next transfer. This is a repeatable cycle, not a chemostat with continuously supplied nutrients. Freezing population samples and ancestors permits comparisons across evolutionary time; the 1994 methods also acclimated cultures before assays, helping separate inherited differences from their immediate prior physiological states.

If the transferred fraction is $q=1/100$ and the batch returns to the same final count, the required population expansion is 100. The number of population doublings per cycle, denoted $G_{\mathrm{cycle}}$, is therefore

\[ G_{\mathrm{cycle}}=\log_2 100\approx6.643856. \]

The familiar “6.6 generations per day” is this convention rounded. It is not a claim that every cell follows exactly 6.6 divisions or that every day's growth is a perfectly synchronous binary tree. Nor does it imply that the population spends the entire day in exponential growth. Daily transfer count, population doublings and elapsed calendar time are related through assumptions about expansion and protocol continuity.

Replicate cultures are independent evolutionary populations, rather than twelve technical measurements of one population. Shared ancestry and matched transfers allow comparison of repeatability; stochastic mutations and lineage histories can produce differences between replicates. Archived population samples can contain multiple genotypes, so a measurement on a population and a measurement on an isolated clone answer different questions. The experiment's present location, custody and generation count can change; the conclusions here concern the specified historical studies rather than an unverified current milestone.

Competition measures realized reproductive performance Lecture 51, 03:18

The lecture speaks of growth rate as a proxy for fitness. In this experiment the stronger operational statement is competitive reproductive performance under the assay conditions. Put an evolved competitor, denoted $E$, and a reference ancestor, denoted $A$, into a common culture, determine their initial and final viable counts, and ask how their relative abundance changes. A maximum exponential growth rate measured in separate cultures can miss differences in lag, yield, depletion response and interactions.

Let $N_i(0)$ and $N_i(T)$ be viable counts of competitor $i$ at the beginning and end of an assay of duration $T$. Define its realized log expansion as $m_i=\ln[N_i(T)/N_i(0)]$. Dividing by $T$ gives an average realized Malthusian rate, meaning a rate of logarithmic population increase. In an assay where the ancestor has positive expansion, a common relative-fitness convention is

\[ w=\frac{m_E}{m_A} =\frac{\ln[N_E(T)/N_E(0)]} {\ln[N_A(T)/N_A(0)]}. \]

This ratio uses the reference as the scale: $w=1$ denotes equal realized log expansion, and $w>1$ denotes the evolved competitor's larger expansion. It is undefined if the reference expansion is zero, so the convention presumes a suitable growth assay. The counts must distinguish competitors and measure the same viable-unit convention. Replicate assays, marker controls and uncertainty estimates matter because counting noise and handling effects can otherwise masquerade as a small advantage.

The connection to frequency change follows directly, without presuming constant exponential rates. Subtracting the two log expansions gives

\[ \ln\!\left(\frac{N_E(T)/N_A(T)}{N_E(0)/N_A(0)}\right) =m_E-m_A=(w-1)m_A. \]

Thus relative fitness is not itself the multiplicative change in abundance odds. If the ancestor expands 100-fold and $w=1.05$, the odds multiplier is $100^{0.05}\approx1.2589$, a roughly 26% odds increase during that assay. The “5% fitness advantage” refers to the log-expansion ratio. Equal starting counts would end at evolved frequency about 0.5573, rather than 0.525 or 0.95.

This identity remains valid for the measured counts even when lag and resource depletion make the growth curve nonexponential. What becomes conditional is extrapolation: the same $w$ need not hold at another initial frequency, medium or transfer schedule. If the competitors alter one another's environment, frequency dependence can matter. The investigator should report the actual comparison, assay setting and whether the sample was a whole population or a clone, rather than treating fitness as a genotype's environment-independent number.

Parallel trajectories can coexist with persistent divergence Lecture 51, 04:32

Once the ancestor remains available as a reference, samples taken at different generations can be compared on a common scale. Typically the ancestral baseline is normalized to one. The LTEE trajectories discussed in the lecture rise rapidly at first and then improve more slowly. Similar broad shapes across replicate populations show repeatable adaptation under the shared regime. They do not imply identical fitness values, identical substitutions, or the disappearance of statistically meaningful variation.

The historical data make the distinction concrete. Lenski and Travisano (1994), p. 6811, Figures 5–7, show an early trajectory measured more densely, broadly parallel changes among populations, and divergence that persisted. The finer sampling reveals steps and pauses that a coarser schedule can smooth over. A smooth population curve can therefore summarize discrete lineage replacements without each microscopic step being smooth.

Multiple useful variants can coexist and compete before one becomes common. Clonal interference is competition between beneficial lineages whose mutations arose independently and cannot readily combine through recombination. It does not guarantee that every episode ends in fixation of exactly one genotype: ecological differences, continued mutation, frequency dependence and limited observation can preserve variation or replace one transient winner with another. Sequencing a successful endpoint can also find neutral hitchhikers, changes carried along because they are linked to a selected variant. Endpoint membership is not, by itself, a measurement of a mutation's beneficial effect.

For the running investigator, parallel fitness trends constrain the response to a shared environment, whereas molecular parallelism concerns recurrent genes, mutations or pathways. Distinct molecular changes can improve similar functions; the same mutation can have different effects in different backgrounds. Fitness assays and genomic histories must therefore be connected through functional tests before an apparent correspondence becomes a causal explanation.

A declining slope does not establish a finite fitness ceiling Lecture 51, 15:04

Let $t$ denote evolutionary time measured in generations and $w(t)$ a fitted relative-fitness trajectory. A positive but decreasing slope means $w'(t)>0$ and $w''(t)<0$ over the fitted interval. These conditions say adaptation is decelerating. They do not determine whether $\lim_{t\to\infty}w(t)$ is finite. Establishing that limit requires more than visual concavity.

Two illustrative descriptions expose the ambiguity. A saturating hyperbola, with total possible increment $a>0$ and time scale $b>0$, is

\[ w_H(t)=1+\frac{at}{t+b},\qquad w_H'(t)=\frac{ab}{(t+b)^2},\qquad w_H''(t)=-\frac{2ab}{(t+b)^3}. \]

It rises with decreasing slope and tends to $1+a$. A power law, with rate-scale parameter $c>0$ and exponent $0<p<1$, is

\[ w_P(t)=(1+ct)^p,\qquad w_P'(t)=pc(1+ct)^{p-1},\qquad w_P''(t)=p(p-1)c^2(1+ct)^{p-2}. \]

It also rises with decreasing slope, but its mathematical limit is infinite. The difference is not a claim that biological fitness can literally grow without bound. It shows that a finite observed interval can support continuing improvement without demonstrating an approaching ceiling. Real energetic, biochemical and ecological constraints can invalidate either phenomenological description outside its useful domain.

The ambiguity is particularly strong early on. Around $t=0$, the hyperbola is $1+(a/b)t-(a/b^2)t^2+\cdots$, while the power law is $1+pc\,t+\tfrac12p(p-1)c^2t^2+\cdots$. Their starting slopes and curvatures can be matched by choosing parameters. A fit and a good plot over the training interval do not decide between their distant extrapolations. Predictions evaluated on later withheld observations supply a more demanding comparison.

Wiser, Ribeck and Lenski (2013), Figures 1–2, tested this issue using later LTEE measurements. Trajectories fit through 20,000 generations with the earlier hyperbolic description underestimated subsequent gains, whereas the power-law description predicted later measurements better. The paper also directly compared samples from 40,000 and 50,000 generations in nine populations, finding a grand mean increase of roughly 3%; three populations were excluded from that particular comparison. That is evidence for continued adaptation in the studied interval, not an experimentally verified infinite-time law.

Return to the investigator's stopping decision. A flattening curve is a reason to improve the measurement and test explicit forecasts, not enough to declare the remaining beneficial mutations exhausted. One should distinguish no detectable gain at the assay's precision from zero underlying gain. Changes in the reference strain or assay conditions would also break the comparability that makes a long trajectory informative.

The available beneficial effects can change with genetic background Lecture 51, 16:13

A distribution of fitness effects, or DFE, specifies the effects of a declared set or sampling process of mutations in a particular genetic background and environment. Two separate changes can slow adaptation: fewer sampled mutations may be beneficial, or the beneficial mutations that remain may have smaller effects. The lecture's changing pie-chart fractions are illustrations of this distinction, not measured percentages for the LTEE.

Diminishing-returns epistasis means that an otherwise comparable beneficial change often has a smaller benefit in an already fitter background. Epistasis is dependence of a mutation's effect on the other genetic changes present. In the model developed by Wiser and colleagues, a decline in accessible beneficial effects can generate continuing but decelerating adaptation. The fact that a power law fits a trajectory does not uniquely identify this mechanism: mutation supply, competition among lineages, ecological interactions and measurement choices can also influence the curve.

A discriminating experiment transfers the same mutation into several ancestral and evolved backgrounds and measures competitive fitness in the same declared environment. If its incremental benefit declines consistently with background fitness, that directly tests diminishing returns for those mutations. Measuring the fraction beneficial requires an appropriate mutation sample and detection threshold; testing only substitutions already selected at an endpoint biases that fraction. An investigator should therefore report the sampling frame, effects and uncertainty, rather than infer a whole DFE from a few successful substitutions.

A mutation-rate modifier can hitchhike with the variants it generates Lecture 51, 20:39

A mutator is a genotype with an elevated mutation rate under a specified comparison, often because a change affects replication fidelity or DNA repair. Its immediate measured growth performance can equal that of another genotype while its descendants sample genetic changes more frequently. In an asexual population, a beneficial descendant can retain the mutation-rate modifier through genetic linkage. Selection on the descendant's beneficial change can then raise the modifier's frequency indirectly.

For a simple mutation-supply comparison, suppose a lineage undertakes $D$ reproductions, has mean total mutation rate $U$ per reproduction, and fraction $f_b$ of its generated mutations belongs to a declared beneficial class. The mean number of such mutation events is $DUf_b$. A modifier multiplying $U$ by $r$ multiplies this mean by $r$ only if $D$ and the effect distribution are otherwise unchanged. It does not guarantee earlier establishment: effects, clone size, stochastic loss and competitors still matter.

The cost side also changes. Deleterious mutations can reduce reproductive performance, and linkage can prevent a modifier from escaping their effects. Purifying selection removes many disadvantaged lineages; it does not make every new deleterious change cost-free, immediately rejected or invisible to a linked modifier. An elevated mutation rate can be directly costly, indirectly favored, or eventually disfavored as the balance changes. Cells do not select a rate by anticipating their future need for adaptation.

Wiser et al.'s 2013 study reported six populations that had evolved substantially elevated point-mutation rates, with about a hundredfold increases in that historical comparison. This is a dated measurement, rather than an invariant count for the continuously evolving experiment. Its existence shows that the mutation process can evolve alongside fitness. The subsequent chapter examines mutation-rate change and fitness landscapes more closely; here the essential conclusion is that the evolving supply of variants complicates any attempt to read a fixed landscape directly from one fitness curve.

Compare two early-matched trajectories without pretending to fit data

The following original calculation makes the extrapolation problem explicit. Time is scaled as $x=t/T$, where $T=20{,}000$ generations. Choose the toy hyperbola $1+0.6x/(x+1)$, which starts with slope 0.6 and curvature −1.2 with respect to $x$. A power law $(1+2.6x)^{3/13}$ has the same starting slope and curvature, because $(3/13)2.6=0.6$ and $(3/13)(-10/13)2.6^2=-1.2$. Matching two local derivatives still leaves different long-run behavior.

The program takes no external inputs, reads no files, modifies no data and uses only Python's standard library. It prints predictions at five declared times; these parameters and outputs are teaching constructions, not fitted LTEE observations. It evaluates nonnegative finite scaled times, rejects other values, and uses ordinary floating-point arithmetic. Very large values can overflow, so this is a finite comparison rather than a universal numerical library.

import math


def trajectories(x):
    """Return two toy relative fitnesses at scaled time x."""
    if not math.isfinite(x) or x < 0:
        raise ValueError("x must be nonnegative and finite")
    hyperbola = 1.0 + 0.6 * x / (x + 1.0)
    power_law = (1.0 + 2.6 * x) ** (3.0 / 13.0)
    return hyperbola, power_law


for generations in (0, 1000, 20000, 50000, 100000):
    scaled_time = generations / 20000.0
    hyperbola, power_law = trajectories(scaled_time)
    print(f"t={generations:6d} H={hyperbola:.6f} P={power_law:.6f}")

The close early predictions make the shared local derivatives tangible. Their later separation identifies the need for withheld observations, but the code itself does not decide which model describes a biological experiment. It also does not supply parameter uncertainty, competition dynamics or a mechanistic DFE.

Exercises

1. Distinguish a culture calendar from population doublings

A population returns to the same final census after each daily 1:100 transfer. How many population doublings does 300 completed cycles represent? What changes if a cycle expands only 80-fold, and why does neither calculation specify every cell's genealogy?

Worked solution

Under matched final and starting counts, each cycle expands 100-fold and contributes $\log_2 100\approx6.643856$ population doublings. Three hundred cycles therefore represent about $1993.1569$ doublings. If expansion is only 80-fold, the cycle contribution is $\log_2 80\approx6.321928$, and a cycle followed by a 1:100 transfer has mean-count multiplier 0.8. Repeated failure to regain the census changes the population trajectory, so the original calendar conversion no longer holds. The population log ratio averages expansion; asynchronous reproduction and deaths can produce heterogeneous individual lineage depths and more divisions than the net expansion alone records.

2. Convert a relative-fitness measurement into frequency change

An evolved strain and its ancestor start at equal viable counts. The ancestor expands 100-fold during competition and the measured log-expansion ratio is $w=1.05$. Calculate the evolved strain's expansion and ending frequency. Would the same conclusion follow if “1.05” instead described the ratio of endpoint counts?

Worked solution

The ancestor's log expansion is $\ln100$. The evolved expansion is $\exp(1.05\ln100)=100^{1.05}\approx125.8925$. Its ending frequency is $125.8925/(125.8925+100)\approx0.5573$, and its abundance odds multiply by $100^{0.05}\approx1.2589$. An endpoint count ratio of 1.05 with equal starting counts would instead give frequency $1.05/2.05\approx0.5122$. It is a different measurement, so the words “5% fitter” need the stated operational convention.

3. Show why concavity cannot prove saturation

For $w_P(t)=(1+ct)^p$ with $c>0$ and $0<p<1$, verify the signs of the first two derivatives and the large-time limit. Compare it with $w_H(t)=1+at/(t+b)$ for positive $a,b$. What observation would distinguish them more strongly than an early fit?

Worked solution

For the power law, $w_P'=pc(1+ct)^{p-1}>0$, while $w_P''=p(p-1)c^2(1+ct)^{p-2}<0$. Nonetheless $w_P(t)\to\infty$ because $p>0$. The hyperbola also has positive first derivative and negative second derivative, but $w_H(t)\to1+a$. Thus identical slope signs do not determine a ceiling. Fit both using a declared early interval and compare predictions with later withheld assays on the same reference scale. Rejecting the hyperbola over that interval supports continued gains there; it does not prove literal biological divergence at infinite time.

4. Test a trade-off without equating physiology with genetic evolution

A descendant culture survives a high-temperature regime that harms its ancestor, but grows poorly at the ancestor's temperature. Design evidence that separates inherited adaptation, acclimation and a performance trade-off. What does Dallinger's historical report contribute and what is missing?

Worked solution

Isolate descendants and ancestors and precondition both in matched common conditions for a declared duration. Measure viable survival and reproduction separately across a temperature range, using independent biological replicates and checking species or strain identity. Persistence after common passage supports inherited differences; disappearance suggests a reversible physiological contribution. A replicated crossing in comparative performance establishes an environment-dependent trade-off under those assays, rather than a universal cost. Genomic comparison and reconstruction can test whether particular sequence changes cause the response. Dallinger's report supplies observations of changed temperature tolerance, pauses and a reversal of tolerance; it lacks the modern controlled genomic and replicated competitive evidence needed to identify a DNA mechanism. The observed organisms and original Fahrenheit values must be retained.

5. Distinguish two changes in the beneficial tail

In a declared mutation sample, an ancestor has beneficial fraction 0.10 and mean effect 0.02 conditional on benefit. A descendant has beneficial fraction 0.02 and conditional mean effect 0.01. For $10^6$ generated mutation events in each background, compare expected beneficial event counts and sums of beneficial effects. Explain why the latter sum is not a prediction of realized adaptation.

Worked solution

The expected counts are $10^6(0.10)=10^5$ and $10^6(0.02)=2\times10^4$. The expected sums of positive sampled effects are $10^6(0.10)(0.02)=2000$ and $10^6(0.02)(0.01)=200$ in the declared effect convention. The tenfold reduction combines a fivefold reduction in the fraction beneficial and a twofold reduction in conditional mean effect. Effects on alternative lineages cannot simply be added to one population's fitness: many mutations are lost, competitors interfere, descendants have changed backgrounds, and effect combinations need not be additive. These original illustrative fractions are not empirical LTEE estimates.

6. Explain the possible rise of a costly mutator

Two linked asexual lineages each undergo $10^8$ reproductions. Their total mutation means per reproduction are $U=10^{-3}$ and $100U$, and each has beneficial fraction $10^{-3}$ for a declared class. Calculate mean beneficial-event supplies. Why does the second number not establish that the mutator will win? Which measurements would test hitchhiking?

Worked solution

The means are $10^8(10^{-3})(10^{-3})=100$ and $10^8(10^{-1})(10^{-3})=10^4$. This comparison fixes reproductive exposure and beneficial fraction; neither is guaranteed equal in an actual mutator experiment. Useful events can have different effects, fail to establish or lose to competing lineages. Elevated deleterious input or a direct modifier cost can reduce exposure and success. Measure mutation rates, competitive fitness of the modifier before additional beneficial changes, and linked lineage trajectories; reconstruct the modifier and candidate beneficial mutations separately and together. A modifier rising with a beneficial descendant while lacking an equivalent initial direct advantage supports hitchhiking under the measured conditions, not purposeful rate adjustment or an inevitable long-term advantage.
