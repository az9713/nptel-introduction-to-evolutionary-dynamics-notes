Innovation, selection, and historical contingency

Lectures 55–57 · Replaying evolution makes historical claims experimentally testable.

An archived bacterium cannot yet grow aerobically on citrate. Its distant descendants can. Another archived bacterium has the same negative phenotype but produces citrate-using descendants much less often when evolution is restarted. The observed starting phenotype is identical; the distributions of accessible futures differ. What does the difference establish, and what further experiment would reveal its molecular cause?

This chapter follows that question from an innovation to a broader experimental design. A replay tests outcomes conditional on a historical starting point. Genetic reconstruction tests mechanisms. Replication separates group differences from variation among repeated futures. We then construct a statistical model for the recording’s selection–chance–history diagrams, keeping an estimated variance distinct from a causal percentage of evolution. The final section changes the transfer protocol to introduce selection for yeast clusters.

1. Compare histories, rather than imagining a second past

Lecture 55, 0:01–16:21

A replay experiment starts independent evolving populations from preserved material and asks how often a specified outcome appears under a stated protocol. Frozen archives give access to historical genotypes, but the restarted populations do not reproduce the exact sequence of random events in the original experiment. Their value is precisely that they sample alternative futures from a known starting state.

Let \(g\) identify a starting genotype, \(E\) an environment and protocol, and \(T\) a duration or opportunity measure. Define \(p_g(E,T)\) as the probability that a replay produces a detectable citrate-utilizing phenotype. A potentiated background has an increased propensity for the specified innovation, relative to a comparison background under those conditions. Potentiation does not require the background already to express the innovation, nor does it mean success is certain. A clone with \(p_g=0.01\) can yield no success in many small replay sets.

A hypothesis of equal opportunity predicts equal \(p_g\) only after controlling other relevant differences. A historically contingent hypothesis predicts that prior changes affect \(p_g\). Cell numbers, generations, resource exposure, incubation, and detection thresholds must be comparable, or explicitly modelled. More cells can produce more opportunities without any molecular change in the per-division rate. A later clone that already expresses the trait is also not an appropriate test of whether an innovation can newly arise.

The 2008 citrate study used clones from different times within the Ara−3 population. Three replay assays differed in scale and conditions: continued serial-transfer evolution and two extensive plate-based screens. All supported a greater propensity in later backgrounds, with 17 independent mutants across the three replay assays. The commonly discussed 19 mutants also includes isolates from subsequent mutation-rate tests. This was not a single experiment restarting all twelve original populations from generation 28,000 and observing 19 successes in one. Blount, Borland, and Lenski, 2008, replay experiments and Tables 1–2.

The defensible conclusion is a change in conditional accessibility over the population’s history. It does not require the final event to be common. A rare actualizing event and a potentiating background can operate together. Nor does failure to observe the phenotype establish a mathematically zero probability. The recording’s sharp contrast between “rare mutation” and “history” is useful as a hypothesis test only when the null means an equally rare opportunity across comparable backgrounds.

2. Zero detections constrain a rate without making it zero

Consider an original simplified experiment with independent detectable events occurring at effective rate \(\mu\) per unit exposure, across total exposure \(D>0\). The exposure might be cell divisions; it must not silently become final cell count when turnover or continued growth makes those different. Under a Poisson approximation, the count \(K\) has mean \(\mu D\), so

\[ \Pr(K=0\mid\mu,D)=e^{-\mu D}. \tag{18.1} \]

If zero events are observed, a one-sided upper confidence bound at level \(1-\alpha\) solves \(e^{-\mu_{\rm upper}D}=\alpha\). Therefore

\[ \mu_{\rm upper}=-\frac{\log\alpha}{D}, \qquad 0<\alpha<1. \tag{18.2} \]

For \(D=10^{10}\) divisions and \(\alpha=0.05\), the bound is \(2.995732\times10^{-10}\) per division. It is an upper bound under the model, not a detected mutation rate, and the confidence statement concerns repeated use of the procedure. Increasing exposure tenfold lowers this zero-event bound tenfold.

Clonal growth complicates counting. One early mutation can leave many colonies: these are not many independent mutation events. Some events fail to grow, establish, or cross the detection threshold. Then the effective detected rate combines molecular occurrence with these later probabilities. Fluctuation assays and the fraction of cultures with no mutants can help separate the timing of occurrence from the final number of mutant cells, but only with their own growth and sampling assumptions. The primary citrate study explicitly discusses such timing and detection complications; (18.1) is a transparent teaching model, not a reanalysis of its raw observations. Blount et al., 2008, replay and mutation-rate analyses.

For a second original comparison, suppose early and late groups each have 20 independent replay populations, with zero and five successes respectively. Under a null of equal success probability, condition on the total five successes. Every set of five successful populations among forty is equally likely. The probability of all five falling in the late group is \(\binom{20}{5}/\binom{40}{5}=0.023562\). Because five is the largest possible late count, this is also the one-sided tail probability. It tests equal binary outcome probabilities under comparable conditions. It does not estimate a per-nucleotide mutation rate or identify a causal potentiating mutation.

3. Regulation can make an existing transporter newly useful

Lecture 55, 16:22–24:14

A promoter is a DNA regulatory region involved in initiating transcription. A protein-coding gene can be present yet insufficiently expressed under particular conditions. A transporter moves substances across a membrane; transport may be limiting even if downstream metabolic reactions already exist. In the citrate case, CitT exchanges citrate with other organic acids. Its ordinary regulation did not provide the aerobic expression needed in the original experimental conditions.

The 2012 genomic analysis identified a tandem duplication that placed a copy of citT under control associated with an aerobically active rnk promoter. This promoter capture changed a regulatory relationship. Reconstruction produced a weak citrate-positive phenotype in a potentiated background, and increased copies strengthened citrate use. Moving the regulatory module into different backgrounds produced different growth responses, supporting epistasis. Replay mutants used several distinct rearrangements rather than a single obligatory duplication boundary. Blount et al., 2012, actualization, refinement, and potentiation analyses.

The three descriptive stages are potentiation, changes that increase accessibility; actualization, appearance of the new phenotype; and refinement, subsequent improvement of its performance. These labels describe roles in the history, not an assertion that every innovation has exactly three mutations or the same unique sequence. A background can change the expression or benefit of an actualizing event without making that event physically impossible elsewhere.

The reconstructed weak phenotype had a small measured competitive advantage, about 1%, in the stated assay. That does not imply an exactly 1% probability of surviving drift. Such a conversion needs a population model, an offspring-variance convention, and a justified mapping from measured competition fitness to its reproduction parameter. The Moran and branching-process approximations earlier in the book have different assumptions. The mechanistic experiment identifies a regulatory change; it does not by itself supply its complete stochastic fate. Blount et al., 2012, reconstruction of the weak citrate-positive phenotype.

Our archived clone’s negative phenotype therefore concealed a changed background. But obtaining the phenotype and exploiting the resource effectively were also distinct. A weak initial function can leave scope for improved expression, transport balance, and growth timing. This is why counting “the mutation that caused citrate use” alone can miss much of the innovation’s history.

4. A selected lineage can carry changes that were previously neutral

Lecture 56, 1:42–12:25

Selection, chance, and history are connected rather than independent substances poured into a population. A neutral allele can be carried to high frequency with a linked beneficial change in an asexual lineage, a process called hitchhiking. The neutral allele need not have increased reproductive success by itself. Random mutation supplies the association; selection can then change the frequencies of the linked genomes.

For a conditional illustration, suppose a deletion disrupts a gene needed for using a resource absent from the present experiment. Assume its direct effect in the current assay is zero. If the deletion-bearing lineage acquires and successfully spreads a beneficial mutation, the deletion can spread with it. After switching resources, the deletion’s consequences can become severe. Neutrality in the first environment is not neutrality in all future environments.

The lecture uses loss of lacZ during growth on glucose as a cartoon. LacZ encodes β-galactosidase, an enzyme involved in lactose breakdown. That example must retain its assumptions: a deletion can affect neighboring genes or impose regulatory consequences, and “unneeded substrate” alone does not prove exact neutrality. Loss of one gene also does not prove an absolute inability ever to evolve some alternative route. The sound historical point is that otherwise similar present phenotypes can carry different inherited capacities for a later environment.

Chance is broader than genetic drift, which is stochastic change in allele frequencies due to reproductive sampling. Random mutation identities and arrival times, the survival of rare lineages, and experimental fluctuations also generate differences between replays. Calling every residual difference “drift” would collapse these mechanisms. An analysis of outcome variation can measure a stochastic component without identifying which stochastic mechanism generated it.

5. Shared changes and dispersed outcomes require careful interpretation

Lecture 56, 12:25–29:56; Lecture 57, 0:01–2:31

Let \(X\) be an ancestral trait value and let \(Y_j\) be the endpoint value in independent replay population \(j\). The trait might be competitive fitness, mean cell size, or a measured metabolic capacity. A plot of \(Y_j\) against \(X\) compares endpoints with the equality line \(Y=X\). The mean shift \(\overline{Y}-X\) and the spread around \(\overline{Y}\) summarize different aspects of the response.

If every replicate shifts similarly, the experiment shows repeatability at the measured resolution. It does not prove that no random mutations occurred or that the trait was directly selected. A pleiotropic effect occurs when a change affects more than one trait. Selection on resource competition can yield a repeatable correlated change in cell size. Conversely, a mean shift of zero does not prove that selection was absent: stabilizing selection can keep a trait near a favored value while still eliminating deviations. Replicates can also experience different beneficial changes whose trait effects cancel in the mean.

The published abstract of Travisano and colleagues’ 1995 study reports similar fitness outcomes in common environments despite prior history and subsequent chance, while cell size was more strongly influenced by history and chance. That is a trait-specific result, rather than a universal rule that all adaptation erases history or that size measures fitness. The statistical construction below develops the lecture’s diagrams; its synthetic values and variance formulas are not presented as that paper’s original dataset or numerical analysis. Travisano et al., 1995, abstract.

6. Replicate futures within distinct historical backgrounds

Lecture 57, 2:31–17:56

To test historical dependence, use several founding backgrounds \(h=1,\ldots,m\), and restart \(n\) independent populations \(j=1,\ldots,n\) from each. The unit of evolutionary replication is the population, not repeated measurements of one endpoint. Match the experimental conditions, randomize positions and handling, and preserve the founders for contemporaneous assays. Otherwise a batch effect associated with a founder can look like a historical effect.

An original balanced random-intercept model is

\[ Y_{hj}=\theta+H_h+C_{hj}, \qquad \mathbb{E}H_h=\mathbb{E}C_{hj}=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(H_h)=\sigma_H^2,\quad \operatorname{Var}(C_{hj})=\sigma_C^2. \tag{18.3} \]

\(\theta\) is the common endpoint mean in trait units. \(H_h\) represents a background-associated deviation, and \(C_{hj}\) variation among futures within that background. Assume independent background effects and independent residuals with equal variance, also independent of one another. These are statistical assumptions. If the particular backgrounds are fixed treatments rather than a sample from a background distribution, a fixed-effects model may be the better target.

Different populations from the same background share \(H_h\), so their covariance is \(\sigma_H^2\). Their group mean has variance \(\sigma_H^2+\sigma_C^2/n\): averaging suppresses residual variation but leaves background variation. This exposes the need for repeated futures within each history. With only one population per founder, background and within-background variation cannot be separated by this design.

Write \(\overline{Y}_h\) for a group mean and \(\overline{Y}\) for the grand mean. The within and between mean squares are

\[ MS_C=\frac{\sum_h\sum_j(Y_{hj}-\overline{Y}_h)^2}{m(n-1)}, \qquad MS_H=\frac{n\sum_h(\overline{Y}_h-\overline{Y})^2}{m-1}. \tag{18.4} \]

Centering each group removes its common \(H_h\); the remaining \(n-1\) independent residual degrees of freedom per group give \(\mathbb{E}MS_C=\sigma_C^2\). The group means contain variance \(\sigma_H^2+\sigma_C^2/n\), giving \(\mathbb{E}MS_H=n\sigma_H^2+\sigma_C^2\). Solving these two expectations yields the method-of-moments estimates

\[ \widehat{\sigma_C^2}=MS_C,\qquad \widehat{\sigma_H^2}_{\rm raw}=\frac{MS_H-MS_C}{n}. \tag{18.5} \]

The raw second estimate can be negative when \(MS_H<MS_C\). A true variance is never negative; a negative raw estimate indicates that this finite sample does not resolve a positive background component by this estimator. Constrained methods can assign zero, but that decision should be stated rather than silently hidden. Normality is not needed for the expectation identities above, although common exact distributional tests add stronger assumptions.

For an original example, take three founders with initial trait values 1.0, 1.2, and 1.4. Their replay endpoints are \(1.28,1.30,1.32\); \(1.48,1.50,1.52\); and \(1.68,1.70,1.72\). Every group mean improves by 0.30. The grand endpoint mean is 1.50, \(MS_C=0.0004\), \(MS_H=0.12\), and the raw history estimate is 0.039867 in squared trait units. Endpoint differences persist between histories even though all have the same mean gain. The mean shift is measured in trait units; these variance components are in squared units. Adding a mean shift and a variance to produce “percent selection” is dimensionally invalid.

7. A variance partition is conditional, not a causal census

In (18.3), \(\sigma_H^2/(\sigma_H^2+\sigma_C^2)\) describes a fraction of modeled endpoint variance associated with the sampled histories. It is not the fraction of mutations caused by history, nor the fraction of total evolutionary change caused by selection. Changing which histories are sampled changes the between-history variance. Extending replay duration can shrink, maintain, or enlarge it. A different trait can give a different result.

Measurement error contributes to within-group variation unless separately estimated. Repeated independent assays of each endpoint can support an additional observation-error component. Unequal residual variances, different cell exposures, or frequency-dependent interactions can require a different model. The residual \(C_{hj}\) cannot be called pure genetic drift without excluding other causes. A variance partition is informative because it makes these assumptions inspectable, not because it eliminates them.

Likewise, convergence of endpoint means does not show that historical differences vanished from every genotype or every future response. It shows convergence for the measured trait, protocol, duration, and resolution. A simple regression \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]=\gamma+\beta X\), with intercept \(\gamma\) and slope \(\beta\), can summarize retained dependence on an ancestral trait: \(\beta=1\) preserves mean spacing, whereas \(\beta=0\) erases that particular linear dependence. But the founding genome contains more history than \(X\). Equal ancestral trait values can still hide the very differences exposed by the citrate replay.

8. Changing transfer changes what selection rewards

Lecture 57, 17:57–30:34

The lecture next asks how to study an initial transition toward multicellularity on a laboratory timescale. A useful experiment should favor a heritable collective property without assuming the collective already exists. Saccharomyces cerevisiae, budding yeast, supplies a tractable starting organism. Faster growth increases representation before transfer; preferential sampling of cells that settle rapidly can impose a second filter. These rewards can conflict.

Ratcliff and colleagues initially let cultures stand for 45 minutes and transferred the bottom fraction. After the first week, the protocol used a brief low-speed centrifugation to make settling selection more efficient. The published experiment thus did not retain an unchanged 45-minute gravity-only step throughout. The question was whether selection for rapid settling would favor heritable clusters, not whether every large object necessarily becomes a multicellular organism. Ratcliff et al., 2012, experimental design.

For an original transfer comparison, let \(G_A,G_B>0\) be the growth multipliers of two types before sampling and let \(0<q_A,q_B\leq1\) be their fractions retained by the transfer filter. Their odds after one cycle are their initial odds multiplied by \((G_B/G_A)(q_B/q_A)\). A type that produces only 80% as many descendants but is retained twice as efficiently gains a net factor 1.6. These are dimensionless cycle multipliers, not maximum instantaneous growth rates. The next chapter derives the competition and cluster-level consequences more fully.

10. Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 18.1 — No events, unequal detection

Zero independent detections occur across \(2\times10^{10}\) divisions. Compute the one-sided 95% upper bound for the effective detected rate. If each true event is detected independently with probability 0.25, what bound follows for the underlying event rate under this thinning model? Explain why counted colonies cannot simply replace independent events.

Solution

The detected-rate bound is \(-\log(0.05)/(2\times10^{10})=1.497866\times10^{-10}\) per division. Independent thinning makes the detected Poisson rate \(0.25\mu\), so the true-rate bound is four times larger, \(5.991465\times10^{-10}\). That inference requires the detection probability to be known, constant, and independent of event timing. Clonal descendants of an early mutation can form many colonies; they do not create many independent origins. Conversely, slow growth or establishment failure can hide true events. If the opportunity count omits continuing divisions or turnover, even the denominator requires correction.

Exercise 18.2 — Condition on successful populations

There are ten early and ten late independent replays, with zero and three successes. Derive the conditional one-sided probability of at least three late successes under equal success probabilities. What does the probability test, and what would unequal exposure change?

Solution

Condition on three total successes among twenty populations. Under the equal-probability null, all \(\binom{20}{3}\) subsets are equally likely. At least three late successes means all three are late, so the tail is \(\binom{10}{3}/\binom{20}{3}=120/1140=2/19=0.105263\). This comparison does not provide strong evidence against equality at a conventional 0.05 threshold; neither does it prove equality. It tests binary success probability per replay. If late populations experienced more divisions, a larger success probability could arise under the same per-division molecular rate. Normalize exposure or model its contribution before attributing a difference to the genetic background.

Exercise 18.3 — Identify a regulatory mechanism

Moving a transporter-regulatory module into a late background yields strong resource use; the same high-copy module in an ancestral background yields weak use. Give experiments that distinguish changed expression, changed downstream metabolism, and changed production of the original rearrangement. Why is the mere presence of the transporter gene insufficient?

Solution

Measure expression with matched reporter constructs and appropriate native-promoter/background controls, then measure protein abundance or transport activity where feasible. Hold expression or transporter dosage comparable while comparing growth and metabolic flux in the backgrounds; persistent differences then point beyond regulatory abundance alone. Measure rearrangement formation with a genotype-based assay that detects the event independently of the growth phenotype, so a poorly expressed event is not mistaken for a missing molecular mutation. Reconstruct candidate background alleles and compare within-background controls to test causality. A coding sequence can be silent under the assay conditions, and expression can still fail to provide effective transport or downstream use. High-copy constructs establish a capability in a manipulation and need not reproduce the initial single-copy event’s phenotype.

Exercise 18.4 — Estimate history without inventing negative variance

Use endpoint groups \((2.0,2.2,2.4)\) and \((3.0,3.2,3.4)\). Compute both mean squares and the raw history variance. Now replace the second group by the first. Interpret the negative raw estimate and explain why a fitted zero would not prove history is irrelevant.

Solution

The group means are 2.2 and 3.2 and the grand mean 2.7. Within each group, squared deviations sum to 0.08, so \(MS_C=0.16/[2(3-1)]=0.04\). Between groups, \(MS_H=3[(2.2-2.7)^2+(3.2-2.7)^2]=1.5\), because its denominator is one. The raw history estimate is \((1.5-0.04)/3=0.486667\). For identical groups, \(MS_H=0\) and \(MS_C=0.04\), giving \(-0.013333\). This is an estimator outcome, not a permissible negative biological variance. A constrained estimate can be zero while uncertainty still allows a positive component. Moreover, the conclusion concerns only this trait, sampled backgrounds, duration, and protocol, and the within component includes any unseparated measurement error.

Exercise 18.5 — A zero mean shift can coexist with selection

An ancestral trait is zero, and replay endpoints are distributed symmetrically around zero. Construct a selection model in which the mean stays zero despite selection acting, and identify an observation that would distinguish it from an unrestricted neutral random walk.

Solution

Let reproductive success decline with squared deviation, for example a relative weight proportional to \(\exp(-az^2)\) with \(a>0\), and let mutational effects be symmetric about zero. Symmetry preserves mean zero, while selection preferentially removes large positive and negative deviations. The distribution can remain concentrated around the optimum instead of acquiring the increasing variance of an unrestricted neutral walk. Measure the association between trait deviation and reproductive success, or experimentally compare the fate of symmetric deviations under controlled conditions. A flat mean alone cannot distinguish stabilizing selection, unbiased drift, or other compensating changes. Parallel means can also hide different underlying genotypes.

Exercise 18.6 — Two filters reverse the growth ranking

A resident produces a 100-fold growth increase and retains 1% of its descendants in a transfer; a clustering variant produces 70-fold growth and retains 2%. Starting with variant frequency 0.10, compute its frequency after one cycle under the original deterministic multiplier model. Why does the result not prove a new level of biological individuality?

Solution

The resident cycle multiplier is \(100(0.01)=1\); the variant’s is \(70(0.02)=1.4\). Initial odds are \(0.1/0.9=1/9\), so final odds are \(1.4/9=7/45\). The frequency is \((7/45)/(1+7/45)=7/52=0.134615\). The transfer advantage more than offsets the growth cost. The model tracks inherited type counts, with fixed multipliers and no stochastic bottleneck or ecological feedback. It says nothing yet about cluster reproduction, heritable collective traits, cell-level conflict, or development. Those observations are needed to interpret selection among multicellular entities rather than mere enrichment of larger particles.

11. Ask what the experiment identifies

Historical contingency becomes tractable when it is phrased as a difference between conditional outcomes, rather than an assertion that an exact past had to occur. Replication identifies patterns; reconstruction and controlled assays narrow their causes. The statistical decomposition is useful only with its units, sampling design, and limitations intact. Changing the transfer filter now offers another way to expose hidden possibilities: a lineage that grows more slowly can still win if its heritable collective structure improves passage to the next cycle.
