Experimental landscapes and evolutionary accessibility

Lectures 25–28 · From mutation orders to measured landscapes

Suppose five genetic changes jointly make a bacterium much more resistant to an antibiotic. There are 120 orders in which those changes can accumulate. Does evolution have 120 equally likely routes, does every blocked route end at a local peak, and would knowing the resistance of all intermediate genotypes settle the prediction? Each inference needs an additional assumption. The lecture sequence turns the landscape of chapter 7 into an experimental object and shows how its measurement and its dynamics determine different kinds of predictability.

Our running construction uses five binary sites, each restricted to an ancestral or chosen derived allele. An original interaction blocks exactly half the shortest uphill orders, although the landscape has only one peak. A second construction has diminishing returns and permits every shortest order. We then ask a different question on an eight-genotype graph: how often does a specified stochastic uphill rule reach each of its two peaks? These teaching examples explain the combinatorics; they do not reproduce the experimental data discussed below.

1. Resistance is a measured phenotype L25 · 3:56

An antibiotic provides a selective environment in which some bacterial variants grow better than others. A resistance phenotype describes performance against a particular antibiotic under specified conditions. It is not an environment-independent property called “fitness.” Different antibiotics impose different molecular constraints, and changing drug concentration can change the ranking of genotypes. The lecture uses resistance as a tractable phenotype for reconstructing mutational routes; this is an experimental question rather than a prescription for antibiotic use.

The minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC, is the lowest tested antibiotic concentration that prevents detectable growth under a stated assay protocol. Concentration has units such as mass per volume. The inoculum, growth medium, incubation time, temperature, and endpoint criterion belong to the measurement. If growth occurs at one tested concentration and fails at the next, the experiment has bracketed a threshold on its concentration grid; it has not measured an arbitrarily precise real number.

MIC does not directly measure killing, nor does it give a per-capita growth rate at every lower concentration. A variant with a higher MIC can still grow more slowly at a particular concentration where both variants grow. Thus an MIC-oriented landscape asks whether each mutation increases an inhibition threshold. A competition-oriented landscape asks whether each mutation improves relative reproductive performance in that competition. Treating these as identical requires an experimentally justified relation between the phenotype and fitness in the chosen environment.

The example introduced in the lecture is the 2006 study of five changes associated with a β-lactamase allele. β-lactamase is an enzyme: a protein that catalyzes a chemical reaction. The study’s primary abstract reports roughly a hundred-thousand-fold difference in resistance and 102 inaccessible orders out of 120, leaving 18. The full 2006 article and supplement were not available for inspection here; these headline facts are supported by the actual indexed primary abstract, and the 18-of-120 result is also explicitly compared in the inspected 2011 primary study below. They should not be read as an independent reanalysis of the 2006 assay or its raw data. Weinreich et al., 2006.

2. Five differences define 32 genotypes and 120 shortest orders L25 · 18:52 L28 · 0:01

Let \(d\) be the number of chosen changes. Label a restricted genotype by a subset \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}\): precisely the sites in \(S\) carry their chosen derived allele. The ancestor is \(\varnothing\); the final combination is the full set. There are \(2^d\) subsets. Connect genotypes differing in one site to obtain a binary cube, with degree \(d\) at every vertex and \(d2^{d-1}\) undirected edges.

A shortest forward mutational order changes every chosen site exactly once, without reversion, and uses no other allele. It is a permutation of \(d\) sites, so there are \(d!\) such orders. For five sites, the 32 genotypes form 80 edges, while 120 different orders reuse those vertices and edges. A route includes six successive vertices, counting both endpoints. Genotype count grows exponentially; the count of these orders grows factorially. The two counts describe different objects.

This restriction is substantial. Five variable nucleotide positions with all four DNA letters would contain \(4^5=1024\) genotypes. A change described at a gene region can also be a deletion or regulatory change, so “five mutations” does not always mean five individual nucleotide substitutions. Reversions, alternative alleles, changes elsewhere in the genome, and longer detours can provide paths outside the restricted binary cube. Counting 120 shortest orders does not count every possible evolutionary history.

Assign a real measured score \(F(S)\) to each vertex. A shortest order is strictly accessible in the uphill approximation when every consecutive step raises \(F\). This approximation treats the population as following successive beneficial substitutions in a fixed environment. It does not assert that deleterious mutations can never arise or fix in a finite population. Ties are excluded by a strict inequality; admitting effectively neutral changes requires a different rule and a declared measurement tolerance.

The hinge in counting accessible orders is that their final step partitions them into disjoint groups. Let \(C(S)\) count strictly accessible forward orders from the ancestor to \(S\). Every such order ends by adding one site \(i\in S\) to its unique predecessor \(S\setminus\{i\}\). Therefore

\[ C(\varnothing)=1,\qquad C(S)=\sum_{i\in S} C(S\setminus\{i\}) \,\mathbf1\{F(S)>F(S\setminus\{i\})\}. \tag{8.1} \]

The indicator is one for an uphill edge and zero otherwise. This recurrence counts orders, not probabilities. It requires \(O(d2^d)\) comparisons, instead of explicitly inspecting all \(d!\) permutations and their steps. If every forward edge is uphill, induction gives \(C(S)=|S|!\). If no uphill first edge exists, every nonempty reachable count is zero.

3. A blocked order does not establish a local peak L25 · 24:26 L26 · 6:37

For two selected sites, use the lecture’s illustrative scores \(F_{00}=1\), \(F_{10}=1.04\), \(F_{01}=0.98\), and \(F_{11}=1.09\). The second change lowers the score by 0.02 in the ancestral background but raises it by 0.05 after the first change. Its effect changes sign. Yet the only peak on this square is 11: 00 can improve to 10, 01 can improve to 00 or 11, and 10 can improve to 11. The unsuccessful order 00→01→11 begins downhill; this says nothing about whether another neighbor of 00 is fitter. A local peak must be checked against every allowed one-change neighbor.

Extend this square to our five-site running construction. Let \(b_j\in\{0,1\}\) be the allele at site \(j\), and add independent positive contributions from the last three sites:

\[ F(b_1,\ldots,b_5)=F_{b_1b_2} +0.02b_3+0.03b_4+0.04b_5. \tag{8.2} \]

The first site always improves the score: by 0.04 when the second is absent, and by 0.11 when it is present. The second site improves it only when the first is already present. Each remaining site always improves it. Consequently a shortest uphill order succeeds exactly when site 1 precedes site 2. Swapping those two labels pairs every permitted permutation with an impermitted one, so 60 of 120 orders succeed.

Nevertheless 11111 is the unique peak. Any missing one of sites 3–5 offers an uphill step. Once those are present, the two-site square still has its unique peak at 11. In fact every vertex has an uphill route to the full combination, even though half the ancestor’s shortest orders fail. This distinction will matter when interpreting the genome-wide experiment: constraints on mutation order can be substantial without trapping an uphill walk at multiple peaks.

Even 18 accessible orders do not imply that each has probability \(1/18\), or that resistance evolution will take one particular route. Mutation supply and fixation probabilities can differ between edges. Under a specified monomorphic substitution model, a path probability is a product of conditional transition probabilities at its intermediate genotypes. The transition probabilities must be normalized over competing available changes at each step. If the environment changes, the fitness orientation may change as well.

4. A reconstructed genome-wide experiment tests another landscape L26 · 16:21 L27 · 5:01

Khan and colleagues reconstructed the 32 combinations of the first five changes fixed in one long-term E. coli population and measured competitive fitness against a marked ancestor. The changes affected rbs, topA, spoT, the glmUS promoter, and pykF, including an rbs deletion. The paper reports about a 30% combined gain, 86 increasing shortest orders, and one peak. Seven edges appeared to decline; one was individually significant, and none survived the reported multiple-testing correction. Khan et al., 2011, pp. 1193–1195.

A promoter is a regulatory DNA region controlling transcription; an operon is a group of genes transcribed together. Distributed changes therefore need not be independent point substitutions within proteins. Edge orientations estimated from noisy assays carry uncertainty, so a comparison based on them should retain that uncertainty. An accessibility count does not assign equally likely histories, regardless of how the mutations were selected.

Physical interaction among substitutions within a protein can constrain their effects, but this comparison cannot establish an intragenic/intergenic law. The two studies select different mutations, measure different performance proxies, and examine different biological systems. Changes in distinct genes can interact through resource allocation or other shared physiology. Mutations chosen because they fixed on successive historical backgrounds are also a selected sample; their behavior does not represent every possible mutation in those gene regions.

Nor can the total endpoint gain be divided by five to infer the five individual effects. On the additive score scale, that operation assumes a particular null model and equal contributions. On a multiplicative scale, five equal factors giving a hypothetical 1.35-fold endpoint would each be \(1.35^{1/5}\approx1.0619\), not 1.07. Neither construction estimates the actual five mutations. Measuring the single mutants and the combinations is precisely what makes an epistasis experiment informative.

5. Diminishing returns need not reverse a mutation’s sign L27 · 27:41

Diminishing returns epistasis means that the advantage of a particular beneficial change tends to become smaller on fitter backgrounds. It is a relation between its marginal effect and background performance. A mutation can remain beneficial everywhere in the tested range while showing diminishing returns. A negative residual against a chosen additive or multiplicative null, a sign reversal, and a declining marginal benefit are related questions but are not equivalent definitions.

In the Khan experiment, four mutations tended to confer smaller benefits on fitter backgrounds, with three individually significant correlations. The pykF change showed the opposite trend and was neutral on the ancestor within the measurement. A tendency with such an exception does not establish a universal saturation rule. Slowing adaptation alone would not prove this mechanism: a population could first acquire larger-effect mutations and later encounter smaller-effect ones without epistasis. Reconstruction helps separate these explanations.

An original model makes the distinction exact. Let \(z=\sum_j v_jb_j\) be a dimensionless latent performance variable with positive increments \(v_j>0\), and define the measured score \(F(z)=1+a(1-e^{-z})\), with \(a>0\). For one additional increment \(v\),

\[ \Delta_vF(z)=F(z+v)-F(z) =ae^{-z}(1-e^{-v})>0. \tag{8.3} \]

Its derivative with respect to the background variable is \(-\Delta_vF(z)<0\), so the benefit decreases continuously with background performance. For two changes \(v,w\), the raw additive residual is

\[ F(z+v+w)-F(z+v)-F(z+w)+F(z) =-ae^{-z}(1-e^{-v})(1-e^{-w})<0. \tag{8.4} \]

Every forward step is still strictly uphill. All 120 shortest orders on five sites succeed, and the all-derived combination remains the unique peak. The model has negative epistasis on the raw score scale and diminishing returns without sign epistasis. Its exponential saturation is a teaching choice, not a fit to the reconstructed bacterial data. At \(z=0\) the marginal gain is largest; as \(z\to\infty\), it tends to zero from above, rather than becoming deleterious.

6. Expanding the alphabet and measuring a pooled library L28 · 6:12

The last lecture expands from selected binary changes to all nucleotide choices in a short region. Papkou and colleagues edited nine nucleotides of the E. coli folA gene, encoding amino acid positions 26–28 of dihydrofolate reductase, abbreviated DHFR. The wild-type amino acids are alanine, aspartate, and leucine. Trimethoprim inhibits DHFR in the study’s selective environment. Nine nucleotide choices give \(4^9=262{,}144\) potential sequences, equivalently \(64^3\) ordered codon combinations because each three-nucleotide codon has 64 possibilities. This count includes synonymous codons and stop codons; it is not a count of 262,144 different functional proteins.

The authors compared variant frequencies before and after selection in six replicate pooled cultures, used sequencing to count variants, and estimated performance relative to the wild type. Their detected before/after library covered 99.7% of the possible sequences. Their Methods describe a count threshold and a regression-based analysis, so the simple two-timepoint equation below is an explanatory model rather than a reproduction of their estimator. Editing nine positions exhausts that designed sequence window, while leaving the rest of the gene and genome fixed. Papkou et al., 2023, article pp. 1, 6–7.

To see what count changes measure, let \(n_v(t)\) and \(n_0(t)\) be sequencing counts for a variant and reference at time \(t\). Assume both counts are proportional to their cell abundances, with variant-specific biases constant across the two samples. Let actual cell counts grow exponentially at constant rates \(g_v,g_0\), in inverse hours, over an interval \(T\) hours. Then dividing their two trajectories eliminates their unknown starting abundances:

\[ \ell_v=\log\frac{n_v(T)/n_0(T)}{n_v(0)/n_0(0)} =(g_v-g_0)T,\qquad \Delta g_v=\frac{\ell_v}{T}. \tag{8.5} \]

The log enrichment \(\ell_v\) is dimensionless. It is a log change in a relative count ratio, not a growth-rate ratio \(g_v/g_0\). The conversion to \(\Delta g_v\) uses the explicit constant-rate model and time interval. With counts 200 versus 100 initially and 600 versus 100 finally, the initial ratio is 2 and the final ratio is 6. Hence \(\ell_v=\log3=1.098612\), and over six hours \(\Delta g_v=0.183102\) per hour. The initial library need not have equal abundances.

If a count is zero, this estimator is undefined. Zero reads may mean a variant was not sampled, not that its biological fitness is exactly zero. Adding a pseudocount changes the estimand and can dominate rare variants; it should be accompanied by sensitivity analysis and an explicit detection model. Time-dependent growth, frequency-dependent competition, or changing amplification bias also breaks the simple interpretation in (8.5). Replication and validation against individually measured variants help test these assumptions.

7. Many peaks can coexist with accessible high endpoints L28 · 24:02

A complete nine-nucleotide Hamming graph has 27 possible one-substitution neighbors per vertex: three alternatives at each of nine positions. The empirical analysis did not treat this entire graph as interchangeable with its usable landscape. It removed nonfunctional variants lacking a functional neighbor, retained nonfunctional variants adjacent to functional ones, and analyzed a largest connected component of 135,178 genotypes. The reported 514 peaks belong to that filtered component. Filtering changes allowed routes and the neighbors against which a peak is defined.

In that analyzed component, most peaks were low. The paper counted 73 peaks above a log-fitness score of 1, then grouped 74 Asp27/Glu27 peaks as “high fitness” by also including one Asp27 peak at 0.87. Its Figures 2–4 and accompanying Results show broad access to high peaks and overlapping basins. An adaptive-walk simulation reached that high group in 76.5% of walks; Methods specify random nonpeak starts and a fixation-probability-based transition rule. This is a simulation outcome on the measured graph, not a count showing that 76.5% of all organisms or all possible gene sequences necessarily evolve resistance. Papkou et al., article pp. 2–5, 7.

To interpret a basin without importing an unjustified geometric picture, fix the directed graph of strictly uphill edges. Define \(\mathcal B_p\), the reachability basin of peak \(p\), as the set of vertices from which at least one uphill path leads to \(p\), including \(p\) itself. These sets can overlap: a vertex can reach several peaks through different choices. They are not a partition like deterministic steepest-ascent basins, where a unique next step and a tie-breaking rule assigns each start exactly one endpoint.

Now give each vertex \(v\) a transition probability \(Q(v,w)\) over its fitter neighbors \(w\), summing to one. In a simple teaching rule, choose uniformly among those neighbors. Let \(H_p(v)\) be the probability of eventually reaching peak \(p\). The first-step decomposition yields

\[ H_p(v)=\sum_{w:F(w)>F(v)}Q(v,w)H_p(w), \quad H_p(p)=1,\quad H_p(q)=0\ \text{for other peaks }q. \tag{8.6} \]

On a finite graph, strict ascent prevents cycles, so every walk stops at a peak. Sorting vertices in decreasing fitness evaluates (8.6) using already computed higher-neighbor values. It also shows that \(\sum_pH_p(v)=1\). Under positive probabilities on every uphill edge, basin membership is equivalent to \(H_p(v)>0\), but it gives no lower bound on that probability. If an allowed uphill edge has zero transition probability, even that equivalence can fail.

For an original binary three-site example, assign the following scores. Binary labels fix the vertex; all one-bit flips are edges.

Genotype000001010011100101110111
Score0210.51.50.71.83

There are two peaks: 001 at height 2 and 111 at height 3. From 000, the uniform rule chooses 001, 010, or 100 with probability one third each. The first choice terminates at the lower peak. Either of the other choices proceeds through 110 to 111, so the higher peak is reached with probability two thirds. Both peaks are accessible from 000, yet neither receives probability one half. A greedy rule instead chooses the highest immediate neighbor, 001, and reaches the lower peak with certainty.

The high peak’s reachability basin contains seven of the eight vertices; the low peak’s contains four. Their overlap contains 000, 011, and 101. Consequently the basin sizes sum to eleven without an error: they count overlapping sets. The experimental lesson is equally precise. Peak number alone does not determine navigability; accessible paths, their transition weights, the start distribution, and the definition of high performance all matter. Predictability of attaining high performance can coexist with unpredictability of the exact terminal genotype.

9. Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 8.1 — Count objects before counting routes

Six chosen changes each have two allowed states. Count genotypes, undirected one-change edges, and shortest forward orders between opposite corners. Then replace the two states by all four DNA letters at six individual nucleotide positions. Which counts change, and when does the shortest-order count remain the same?

Solution

The binary cube has \(2^6=64\) vertices, degree 6, and \(64\cdot6/2=192\) edges. Its opposite corners differ at all six sites, giving \(6!=720\) shortest orders. The DNA graph has \(4^6=4096\) vertices, degree 18, and \(4096\cdot18/2=36{,}864\) edges. If the specified endpoint differs from the ancestor at exactly six sites, every shortest path changes each directly to its specified endpoint letter once, so the count remains 720. If they differ at only \(h\) sites, it is \(h!\). Alternative intermediate letters create longer paths, not new shortest paths between the same endpoints.

Exercise 8.2 — An order barrier without a trap

Use (8.2). Prove that every genotype can reach 11111 uphill, count shortest accessible ancestor-to-endpoint orders, and identify why calling each inaccessible order a separate local peak is incorrect.

Solution

Add any missing sites 3–5; every addition is positive. If site 1 is missing, add it: its effect is 0.04 or 0.11, depending on site 2. Finally add site 2 if missing, gaining 0.05 after site 1. This constructs an uphill path from every genotype. A shortest order from 00000 must place site 1 before site 2, since site 2 alone loses 0.02; this condition is also sufficient. Exchanging their positions is a bijection between the two order classes, so there are \(5!/2=60\) accessible orders. An inaccessible order prescribes a bad next choice. A peak says every possible next one-change choice fails. The existence of a better unchosen neighbor prevents that conclusion.

Exercise 8.3 — What does a combined gain identify?

A hypothetical five-change genotype has fitness 1.35 relative to an ancestor of fitness 1. Compare the equal additive and equal multiplicative explanations. Explain why neither identifies five measured mutation effects, and design the necessary background comparison for one mutation.

Solution

Equal additive effects assign each change 0.35/5=0.07 on the raw scale. Equal multiplicative factors assign \(r=1.35^{1/5}=1.061858\), an effect of about 6.19% per factor. Both produce the same endpoint under their own null model, demonstrating nonidentification from a single combined observation. Unequal factors and interacting effects can also produce it. For mutation \(j\), construct matched pairs \(S\) and \(S\cup\{j\}\) for all 16 backgrounds among the other four mutations, assay them with replicates, and estimate a difference or ratio on a declared scale. That pairing tests its background dependence while holding other alleles fixed. The paper’s empirical gain should not be replaced by this hypothetical1.35.

Exercise 8.4 — Relative counts require a baseline

A variant/reference ratio changes from 200/100 to 600/100 over six hours. Derive the constant-rate difference and explain why using only the final ratio gives the wrong answer. What happens if the final variant count is zero?

Solution

The ratio of ratios is 3, so \(\ell=\log3=1.098612\) and \(\Delta g=\log3/6=0.183102\) per hour. Using \(\log6/6=0.298627\) per hour wrongly attributes the initial twofold abundance excess to growth. The reference removes common sampling depth, while the initial ratio removes unequal starting abundance. With a zero final count the logarithm is undefined; finite sampling does not establish a biologically infinite negative growth difference. Report a detection limit or fit an explicit count model rather than silently adding a pseudocount. The constant-rate conversion also needs the assay’s actual elapsed time.

Exercise 8.5 — Negative epistasis with every order accessible

In (8.3)–(8.4), set \(a=0.5\) and consider two increments \(v=w=0.2\) on background \(z=0\). Calculate the first and second marginal effects and their additive residual. Does the residual block either order?

Solution

The first marginal gain is \(0.5(1-e^{-0.2})=0.090635\). After the first change, the second contributes \(0.5e^{-0.2}(1-e^{-0.2})=0.074205\). Their difference, \(-0.016429\), is the additive residual \(-0.5(1-e^{-0.2})^2\). Both gains are positive, and symmetry gives the same values in reversed order. Negative epistasis reduces the second gain but blocks neither route. With any number of positive increments, every forward edge remains positive; diminishing returns alone need not generate sign reversals, inaccessible orders, or multiple peaks.

Exercise 8.6 — Basin membership versus endpoint probability

For the eight-genotype table, verify the two basin sizes and their overlap. Compute the high-peak endpoint probability for a start chosen uniformly over all eight vertices under the uniform uphill rule. Compare the result with a greedy rule starting at000.

Solution

The low peak001 is reachable from001,000,011,101, giving four basin members. The high peak111 is reachable from every vertex except001, giving seven. Their intersection is000,011,101, of size three; inclusion–exclusion gives \(4+7-3=8\). At000,011,101 there are three fitter neighbors and exactly one leads directly to001; the other two eventually reach111, giving high probability2/3 each. At010,100,110,111 the high probability is one, and at001 it is zero. The uniform-start average is \((3\cdot2/3+4)/8=3/4\). Merely counting the high basin would give7/8, which answers existence of a route rather than its probability. The greedy rule from000 chooses001, score2, over010 and100, and therefore has high-peak probability zero. Geometry, dynamics, and starting distribution are distinct inputs.

10. What an experimental landscape can predict

The running five-site construction separated blocked orders from blocked destinations. The concave construction separated diminishing benefits from sign reversals. The eight-genotype construction separated reachability from probability. Each is a precise way to ask what a resistance landscape or a competition experiment has established.

A measured landscape supplies vertex values with uncertainty on a specified graph in a specified environment. An evolutionary prediction additionally supplies mutation opportunities, population dynamics, and a starting distribution. Dense experimental coverage can greatly constrain those predictions, but it cannot turn an assay-specific score into a universal fitness function. The next chapters move from these geometric restrictions to the mechanisms by which variation is generated and populations change.
