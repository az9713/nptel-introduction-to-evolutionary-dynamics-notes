Mutation rates, future adaptation, and landscape peaks

Lectures 52–54 · A population can improve more slowly without having stopped improving.

Two bacterial clones are sampled from an evolving population. Clone A grows better in today’s competition assay; clone B leaves descendants that improve more during the next several hundred generations. Which clone should win? The question has two clocks. Present competitive fitness determines the initial change in their frequencies. Future mutation, genetic background, and the mutations that survive selection determine how that comparison may change. Neither clock can be read from the other.

Our running case is an original pair of asexual lineages in a fixed environment, followed by a second question about their long trajectory: does slowing improvement imply an approaching ceiling? We will distinguish current fitness, mutation supply, conditional future improvement, and a fitted curve’s mathematical limit. The experimental examples supply evidence for these distinctions; the probability race and matched curves below are teaching constructions, not reconstructions of measured bacterial data.

1. A mutation-rate modifier can benefit through its descendants

Lecture 52, 0:01–7:35

A mutator is a genotype with an increased mutation rate in a specified comparison. Increasing that rate does not by definition increase its immediate growth rate. A modifier allele changes some aspect of the production or inheritance of variation; its effect on fitness can be direct, indirect, or both. In an asexual population, descendants tend to inherit a modifier together with subsequent beneficial mutations. Their success can carry the modifier to higher frequency. This is an indirect association with successful descendants, not selection anticipating a future need.

Let A have the ordinary mutation rate and B carry a modifier. Write \(x=B/(A+B)\) for B’s current frequency. Initially suppose the two have identical reproductive performance, so B’s relative reproductive fitness is \(r=1\). Introduce a multiplier \(m\geq0\) for the supply of successful beneficial mutations per lineage member. This is more restrictive than a multiplier for all molecular mutations: the beneficial fraction and establishment probabilities must also match if a total mutation-rate multiplier is to equal \(m\). Until the first successful mutation appears, hold \(x\) fixed and approximate the two supplies by independent Poisson processes with rates

\[ \lambda_A=c(1-x),\qquad \lambda_B=cmx, \qquad c>0. \tag{17.1} \]

Here \(c\) has units of inverse generations and incorporates the total population size, ordinary beneficial supply, and establishment probability. “Successful” means passing a stated initial establishment criterion; it does not yet mean eventual fixation. The first B event occurs at time \(t\) with density \(\lambda_Be^{-\lambda_Bt}\), while no A event has occurred with probability \(e^{-\lambda_At}\). Multiplication and integration give

\[ \Pr(\hbox{first successful event is in B}) =\int_0^\infty\lambda_Be^{-(\lambda_A+\lambda_B)t}\,dt =\frac{mx}{1-x+mx}. \tag{17.2} \]

The hinge is conditioning on a competition between mutation arrivals, rather than competing present growth rates. For \(x=0.01\) and \(m=100\), B supplies the first such event with probability \(1/1.99=0.502513\), despite representing only 1% of the cells. With \(m=1\), (17.2) reduces to \(x\); with \(m=0\) and \(x<1\), it is zero. If both supplies vanish, there is no first event and the conditional question is undefined.

This probability is not B’s eventual fixation probability. The mutation may have a small benefit, competitors may acquire other beneficial mutations, and B may bear a direct cost or increased deleterious load. Recombination can separate the beneficial mutation from its modifier, weakening the association. If B’s frequency changes appreciably during the waiting period, constant rates in (17.1) are inappropriate; the arrival calculation must use time-dependent hazards. The neutral fixation result \(x\) from the finite-population chapter answers a different question under a different model.

2. Measuring future improvement requires a replay experiment

Lecture 52, 7:36–27:44

Evolvability concerns a genotype or population’s capacity to generate heritable changes with consequences in a specified setting. For an operational comparison, fix an environment, a transfer protocol, a population size regime, a future duration \(\Delta t\), and a fitness assay. If \(W_g(t)\) is the dimensionless measured fitness of a descendant population initiated from genotype \(g\), one possible summary is \(\mathbb{E}[W_g(\Delta t)-W_g(0)]\), averaged over replicated histories. Changing the duration or environment can change the ranking. A single endpoint cannot establish a universal property called “more evolvable.”

Present fitness and this future gain need not correlate positively. A genotype can sacrifice an immediate benefit while opening a favorable set of later changes. Conversely, today’s best genotype can make certain beneficial changes redundant. Genetic background enters both the distribution of mutational effects and the supply of changes that are accessible. Increased molecular mutation rate is one possible cause of changed future gains, not their definition.

Woods and colleagues studied clones from generation 500 of the long-term E. coli experiment. The lineages later labelled eventual winners began about 6.3% less fit than eventual losers. Forty replay populations—ten from each of four clones—were evolved for 883 generations; winner-derived populations had a higher mean endpoint fitness. Each replay initially mixed neutral marker variants of its own founding genotype, rather than placing winner and loser genotypes together in every replay. The result concerns replicated tendencies, not an assertion that every initially inferior clone always wins. Woods et al., 2011.

The study also reconstructed mutations to investigate mechanism. A spoT mutation beneficial in the ancestral and winner backgrounds lost that benefit in a loser background: this is negative epistasis in that background, not evidence that the winner necessarily had positive epistasis. Multiple changes contributed, and sequencing did not support a higher mutation rate as the explanation for the winner advantage. Whole clones differed at more than one relevant locus; their comparison cannot be reduced to a universal two-single-amino-acid contest. The inspected primary manuscript supplies these qualifications to the recording’s simplified narrative. Woods et al., 2011.

The experimental inference has a useful structure. A replay from an archived genotype tests a conditional distribution of futures from that genotype. Allele reconstruction then tests whether a particular background change causes a particular effect. The first establishes a reproducible difference in outcomes; the second helps identify its cause. Neither substitutes for the other. In our running case, today’s frequency advantage and tomorrow’s expected improvement must therefore be estimated separately.

3. Two slowing trajectories have incompatible limits

Lecture 53, 0:01–19:54

Let \(t\) denote elapsed generations and let \(W(t)\) be fitness relative to a declared reference, normalized to \(W(0)=1\). It is dimensionless. A decreasing derivative means smaller gains per generation, not necessarily a finite limiting fitness. Consider two candidate forms:

\[ H(t)=1+\frac{at}{b+t}, \qquad P(t)=(1+\kappa t)^p, \quad a,b,\kappa>0,\quad 0<p<1. \tag{17.3} \]

\(a\) and \(p\) are dimensionless, \(b\) is measured in generations, and \(\kappa\) has units of inverse generations. The parameter \(b\) is the time at which the hyperbola has realized half its eventual gain \(a\). Both start at one. Direct differentiation gives

\[ H'(t)=\frac{ab}{(b+t)^2},\quad H''(t)=-\frac{2ab}{(b+t)^3}; \qquad P'(t)=p\kappa(1+\kappa t)^{p-1},\quad P''(t)=p(p-1)\kappa^2(1+\kappa t)^{p-2}. \tag{17.4} \]

Each first derivative is positive and each second derivative negative. Yet \(H(t)\to1+a\), while \(P(t)\to\infty\). For the power law, \(P'(t)\to0\) even though the accumulated gain is unbounded. An arbitrarily small positive rate can integrate to an infinite total. The restriction \(p<1\) is essential for slowing: \(p=1\) gives constant improvement, and \(p>1\) gives acceleration.

To expose why a short record can be ambiguous, construct the curves with the same initial slope \(\sigma>0\) and the same initial curvature. From (17.4), impose \(a/b=p\kappa=\sigma\) and \(2\sigma^2/a=(1-p)\sigma^2/p\). Hence

\[ a=\frac{2p}{1-p},\qquad b=\frac{a}{\sigma},\qquad \kappa=\frac{\sigma}{p}. \tag{17.5} \]

Their Taylor expansions now agree through the quadratic term. This is a local mathematical matching condition, not a regression against experimental observations. With \(p=0.1\) and \(\sigma=10^{-5}\) per generation, \(a=2/9\), \(b=22{,}222.22\) generations, and \(\kappa=10^{-4}\) per generation. At 20,000 generations the values are 1.105263 and 1.116123. At one million they are 1.217391 and 1.586471. The discrepancy grows despite matching initial value, slope, and curvature. An excellent short-range fit cannot by itself identify the limiting behavior.

4. Future observations test a fitted law more sharply

Lecture 53, 20:03–29:10; Lecture 54, 0:01–8:17

A training fit estimates parameters using the observations supplied to it. A holdout prediction freezes those parameters and compares the predicted trajectory with later observations. Refitting after seeing the later observations answers another question. In adaptation experiments, replicated lineages and measurements of uncertainty matter because a smooth curve describes a population summary while individual histories can have bursts, delays, and different endpoints.

Wiser, Ribeck, and Lenski compared hyperbolic and power-law descriptions of relative fitness in the long-term experiment through 50,000 generations. Fits based on the first 20,000 generations were compared with later measurements: the hyperbolic model systematically underestimated subsequent improvement, and the power law predicted it better. Figure 2 distinguishes fitted early observations from later observations and their predictions. This supports that model comparison over that experiment and time range; it does not observe an infinite future. Their fitness measure uses realized growth in competitions, rather than simply the inverse of exponential doubling time. Wiser et al., 2013, Figure 2 and accompanying text.

Model comparison should retain the measured quantity. A relative competition fitness of 1.5 need not mean a strain’s maximum instantaneous growth rate is 50% higher: lag, growth phases, and the assay’s transfer conditions contribute. A correlation coefficient is also not automatically an \(R^2\), and neither statistic by itself proves a model mechanism. The recording’s broad lesson survives these distinctions: later observations can separate shapes that were difficult to discriminate from the earlier record.

There is a second uncertainty beyond parameter error. If both fitted families omit a changing resource, a new phenotype, or frequency-dependent coexistence, both can fail together. A narrow confidence interval inside the wrong family does not include that discrepancy. Our matched curves illustrate the first ambiguity—different limits from similar local behavior—but cannot quantify experimental noise or choose the correct family for real cells.

5. An original rate model produces slowing without a ceiling

A phenomenological curve summarizes observations; a dynamical model proposes how a rate depends on state. To connect these levels without pretending to derive all microbial adaptation, suppose an original coarse model obeys

\[ \frac{dW}{dt}=K W^{-q},\qquad W(0)=1,\quad K>0,\quad q>0. \tag{17.6} \]

\(K\) has units of inverse generations and \(q\) is dimensionless. The right side says improvement becomes slower as fitness rises but never becomes zero at a finite \(W\). It could summarize longer waiting times for successful increments, smaller increments, or both. The equation alone does not identify which mechanism applies. It ignores discrete mutations, interference between competing clones, fluctuation between transfers, and population-level stochasticity.

Separate variables: \(W^q\,dW=K\,dt\). Integrating from the initial condition yields \((W^{q+1}-1)/(q+1)=Kt\), and therefore

\[ W(t)=\bigl[1+(q+1)Kt\bigr]^{1/(q+1)}. \tag{17.7} \]

This has the power-law form with \(p=1/(q+1)\) and \(\kappa=(q+1)K\). The curvature is negative because \(d^2W/dt^2=-qK^2W^{-2q-1}\). For \(q=0\), the solution becomes \(1+Kt\) and slowing disappears; for \(K=0\), fitness remains one. The code accepts these boundary cases but the slowing interpretation requires both \(K>0\) and \(q>0\).

The empirical study motivates investigating processes such as diminishing returns and competition among beneficial lineages. Equation (17.6) is our simplified construction, not the authors’ complete mechanistic model or an inferred law for their populations. A decreasing mutational increment is sufficient to motivate slowdown in some models, but observing slowdown cannot uniquely diagnose epistasis: changing mutation supply and the depletion of initially available large-effect mutations can also reduce gains.

6. A finite landscape and a finite observation window answer different questions

Lecture 52, 27:44–31:13; Lecture 54, 3:10–12:21

For a fixed DNA length \(L\), there are \(4^L\) sequences. If each sequence has a fixed real-valued score in one unchanging environment, a global maximum exists because the set is finite. A walk that moves only to a strictly higher-scoring sequence cannot revisit a previous sequence. It therefore makes at most \(4^L-1\) moves before reaching a vertex with no strictly better permitted neighbor. This is a statement about a single-genotype walk on a fixed graph. Its endpoint may be a local peak far below the global maximum.

Several assumptions prevent transferring that argument directly to every evolving population. Genome length can change; structural variants enlarge a chosen state space; ecology can change the payoff of a genotype; and frequency dependence makes a single fixed score inadequate. A polymorphic population is not a single vertex. Even on a genuinely fixed finite landscape, neutral substitutions can continue at a fitness plateau, and mutation–selection balance can retain changing genetic variation. A limit on fitness is not a limit on all genetic change.

There is therefore no contradiction between a power law fitting an experimental interval and a biological score having a ceiling outside that interval. The fitted formula’s unbounded continuation is a property of the formula. To claim that continuation indefinitely would require the model’s conditions to persist indefinitely. Conversely, the existence of a finite ceiling gives no estimate of where the observed population lies relative to it. Slowing alone establishes neither that a peak has been reached nor its height.

The distinction is especially important for physical extrapolation. Wiser and colleagues discussed a very distant power-law extrapolation of roughly 4.7-fold relative fitness at 2.5 billion generations and translated it while accounting for ancestral lag as well as doubling time. This was an illustrative extrapolation, not a measured future phenotype. Dividing an ancestral doubling time directly by a competition-fitness ratio silently discards the lag and assay conventions. A calculation that seems physically implausible should first be checked for those conversions before it is used to reject a finite-window fit. Wiser et al., 2013, discussion.

7. A new resource can change the question of what remains possible

Lecture 54, 12:21–31:33

The long-term experiment’s medium contained low glucose and citrate. Citrate helped the medium’s chemistry and was an abundant potential carbon resource, but the ancestral strain could not use it for growth under the experiment’s aerobic conditions. A resource can be present without being physiologically accessible: transport, regulation, and metabolism are separate requirements. “Cannot use citrate aerobically in this assay” is more precise than “E. coli can never metabolize citrate.”

Blount, Borland, and Lenski found citrate-utilizing descendants in one experimental population by generation 31,500, with an unusual density increase noticed later. Marker and sequence checks supported descent within the experiment rather than contamination. These dated observations concern the tested lineage and conditions. They do not measure a universal probability of innovation. Blount et al., 2008, introduction and emergence of Cit+ mutants.

Two hypotheses now compete without being mutually exclusive. Perhaps the necessary mutation was simply extraordinarily rare in the ancestor. Perhaps earlier changes made its occurrence or expression more likely, so later genetic backgrounds were potentiated for this innovation. A rare event can still require a particular background. Archive samples let the experimenter restart evolution from different historical points and compare the resulting probabilities; the next chapter develops that inference.

Return to our running case. A clone’s present score, its probability of supplying tomorrow’s beneficial mutation, and its ability to access a previously unused resource are three different objects. A landscape projection that records only current growth can hide the second and third. Evolutionary forecasting consequently requires a stated state space and assay, a model of accessible variation, and tests of the model on histories it did not fit.

9. Exercises with worked solutions

Exercise 17.1 — A first arrival is not a lineage’s fate

A modifier is at frequency 0.02, doubles successful beneficial supply, and has equal present growth to the resident. Calculate its probability of supplying the first established beneficial change under (17.1). Compare with its neutral fixation probability without ongoing mutation. Then let its frequency decline as \(x(t)\). Write the correct time-dependent first-arrival expression, and explain why inserting only \(x(0)\) is insufficient.

Solution

The arrival probability is \(0.04/(0.98+0.04)=2/51=0.039216\). Under the no-mutation neutral finite-population model, eventual fixation is instead 0.02. The event in the first calculation can alter later fitness and competition, so these probabilities need not agree. If \(\lambda_A(t)=c[1-x(t)]\) and \(\lambda_B(t)=2cx(t)\), the probability that the first event is a B event is the integral over \(t\geq0\) of \(\lambda_B(t)\exp[-\int_0^t(\lambda_A(u)+\lambda_B(u))\,du]\). If there is a positive probability of no event ever, divide by the probability that some event occurs when asking a conditional first-event question. A declining modifier supplies fewer future trials; its starting frequency does not describe the whole waiting interval. This still omits competition after establishment and deleterious load.

Exercise 17.2 — Separate background effects from mutation rate

Clone A starts fitter, but B-derived populations have greater mean gains after replicated evolution. Design comparisons that distinguish an increased mutation rate in B from a background-dependent benefit of mutation \(j\). Why do endpoint fitness comparisons alone identify neither explanation, and why should a result reported as a mean not become “B always wins”?

Solution

Use independent populations founded from A and B with matched sizes, transfers, environment, and elapsed generations; retain their full endpoint distributions. Separately estimate mutation rates with a stated assay, including uncertainty and the mutation class it detects. Reconstruct \(j\) in both backgrounds, alongside sham-reconstructed controls, and measure its effect within each background using the same fitness protocol. Equal molecular rates with a reduced or absent \(j\) benefit in A support a background effect for that mutation; higher rates in B do not prove that those additional mutations caused the fitness difference. Sequencing can identify repeated changes but recurrence also reflects selection. Endpoint means combine supply, effect distributions, establishment, and competing lineages, so they cannot separate these quantities alone. A difference in means can coexist with overlapping distributions and individual histories in the opposite order; an “always” claim requires evidence the experiment does not supply.

Exercise 17.3 — Match curvature, then compare ceilings

Derive (17.5) from equal initial slopes and curvatures. Use \(p=1/4\), \(\sigma=0.002\) per generation. Find \(a,b,\kappa\), evaluate both curves at \(t=b\), and determine their limiting values. Check whether matching the first two derivatives identifies the limit.

Solution

The slope equations give \(b=a/\sigma\) and \(\kappa=\sigma/p\). Substituting into the second derivatives gives \(-2\sigma^2/a=(p-1)\sigma^2/p\), hence \(a=2p/(1-p)\). Thus \(a=2/3\), \(b=333.333\) generations, and \(\kappa=0.008\) per generation. At \(b\), \(H=1+a/2=4/3\), while \(P=(1+8/3)^{1/4}=(11/3)^{1/4}\approx1.383783\). The hyperbola tends to \(5/3\); the power law has no finite limit. The matching conditions fix local derivatives and do not constrain the infinite continuation. The positive initial slope has units of inverse generations; omitting those units would make the numerical \(b\) uninterpretable.

Exercise 17.4 — Derive an implied improvement mechanism

Take \(dW/dt=0.003W^{-2}\), \(W(0)=1\), with generations as time units. Solve it, calculate the time to \(W=2\), and find the ratio of instantaneous improvement rates at \(W=2\) and \(W=1\). Does this demonstrate diminishing-returns epistasis in a real population?

Solution

Integrating \(W^2dW=0.003dt\) gives \(W^3=1+0.009t\), so \(W=(1+0.009t)^{1/3}\). At \(W=2\), \(t=(8-1)/0.009=777.778\) generations. The rate ratio is \(2^{-2}=1/4\). The model is slowing and unbounded: the time to every finite \(W\) is finite, but required times grow. It describes a decreasing aggregate improvement rate. It does not distinguish smaller mutation effects from longer waits, interference, or another process. Demonstrating epistasis would require measuring the same mutation’s effect on controlled backgrounds, rather than inferring that cause uniquely from this trajectory.

Exercise 17.5 — What does a finite genome prove?

A model contains all fixed-length DNA sequences of length \(L\), with a fixed score and a fixed one-change neighbor graph. Prove termination of a strictly uphill single-genotype walk. Explain why this does not prove arrival at the global maximum, why genetic change can continue at a plateau, and why a fitted unbounded law can remain useful.

Solution

There are \(4^L\) vertices. A strictly increasing score prevents revisiting any vertex, so a walk has at most \(4^L-1\) transitions and ends with no permitted fitter neighbor. That endpoint is a local maximum; disconnected uphill routes or valleys can prevent reaching the globally best vertex. If equal-score moves are admitted, the strict-increase proof no longer excludes continued movement among neutral genotypes. A population with recurrent mutation can also change its composition at a stationary mean score. Changing genome length or ecology changes the assumed model itself. Finally, an unbounded analytic curve can approximate observations far below the finite maximum over a restricted time interval. Its useful interpolation and holdout predictions do not entail the truth of its infinite extrapolation.

Exercise 17.6 — Test historical access to an unused resource

A citrate-utilizing lineage appears once in a long experiment. Propose an archived-sample replay that distinguishes a uniformly rare ancestral opportunity from a historical increase in the chance of producing the phenotype. State the unit of replication, essential controls, and a conclusion that would remain unjustified even if late samples produced the phenotype more often.

Solution

Restart many independent populations from archived ancestral, early, and late genotypes under the same aerobic medium and transfer regime. Normalize starting cell numbers and track cumulative divisions or another justified exposure, since unequal opportunity can change success probabilities without potentiation. Each independently evolving population is a replicate; repeated measurements of one population are not new histories. Include identity and contamination checks, direct phenotype assays, marker controls, and archived controls that verify citrate does not already support growth at the start. A reproducible late-background increase supports historical dependence of the phenotype’s occurrence or expression under this protocol. It does not by itself identify the causal allele, establish that the actual historical path was necessary, or prove that a late founder inevitably produces the phenotype. Reconstruction and mechanistic experiments are needed to distinguish an altered mutation event from an altered effect of the same event.

10. Forecast the conditional process

Present competitive rank is a state measurement; future adaptation is a distribution conditioned on that state and on the experiment. Slowing is a derivative; a ceiling is a limit. A mutation supply race is a first-event calculation; fixation is a later population outcome. Keeping these objects distinct makes it possible to learn from replay experiments and trajectory fits without demanding that either predict every future. The citrate example now turns the same discipline toward historical contingency: compare accessible futures, then identify what changed their accessibility.
