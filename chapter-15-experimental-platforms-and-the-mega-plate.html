Experimental platforms and the MEGA-plate

An investigator wants to know how bacteria acquire antibiotic resistance. Repeated liquid transfers, a continuously fed vessel, randomly chosen colonies, and a spatial drug gradient can all yield changed genomes. They do not expose the same evolutionary process or answer the same question. The apparatus controls which variants reproduce, which lineages survive sampling, and which intermediates can reach the next environment.

Our original running construction is a choice among these platforms for the same ancestral strain. The investigator has three possible objectives: measure adaptation to a repeating environment, estimate the mutations that arise with reduced selective filtering, or observe the spatial paths to resistance. Lectures 47–49 compare the first three methods and then introduce Baym and colleagues' MEGA-plate. We will turn their schematic arguments into explicit event, sampling, and transport models, and distinguish the lecture's concentration cartoon from the primary paper's measurements.

A serial transfer imposes a growth-and-sampling cycle Lecture 47, 0:01

In serial subculture, cells grow in one batch of medium for an interval $\Delta t$, and a small aliquot seeds a fresh batch. Let $d>1$ be the dilution factor, so the transferred fraction is $q=1/d$. A 1:1000 dilution means that one thousandth of the previous culture contributes to the new culture under the idealized mixing convention. The number of transferred cells remains stochastic; volume fraction is not an exact count of founder cells.

If each batch expands from $N_0$ to $dN_0$ and the transfer restores the expected starting size, the population doublings per cycle are

\[ G_{\mathrm{cycle}}=\log_2 d. \]

Thus 1:1000 gives 9.965784 doublings, approximately ten, whereas 1:100 gives 6.643856. Fifty 1:1000 cycles give about 498.29 doublings under complete regrowth, rather than exactly 500. The conversion presumes that the actual expansion compensates the dilution. Repeating a nominal volume transfer does not ensure that it has done so.

To expose that condition, let $A_j$ be the actual expansion factor in batch $j$, and let $n_j$ be its starting count. Ignoring demographic randomness and using the expected transferred count, the cycle map is

\[ n_{j+1}=\frac{A_j}{d}\,n_j. \]

A constant exponential net growth rate $g$ over the full growth interval would give $A=e^{g\Delta t}$, hence the persistence condition $g\Delta t\ge\ln d$. If growth begins only after an illustrative lag $\ell$, the corresponding condition is $g\max(\Delta t-\ell,0)\ge\ln d$. These are scheduling approximations, not promises of indefinite exponential growth: nutrient depletion caps actual expansion. A culture transferred before recovery, or a culture with inadequate nutrient-supported yield, can fail despite a high measured maximum growth rate.

Equal generation counts do not imply equal mutation supply Lecture 47, 9:56

Suppose the smaller culture ends each cycle with $K$ cells and starts with $qK$, with binary fission and no losses. Its division count is $K(1-q)$. If the mean number of counted mutations per division is $U$, expected event supply per cycle is

\[ \mathbb E[M_{\mathrm{event}}]=U K(1-q). \]

For $q=10^{-3}$ and $U=10^{-3}$, final counts of $10^{10}$ and $10^{11}$ produce illustrative expectations of $9.99\times10^6$ and $9.99\times10^7$ events. Both cultures complete the same population doublings, yet the larger supplies ten times as many events. This uses the same assumed rate and organismal conditions in both vessels; real differences in oxygenation, density, or growth state can invalidate that equality.

Mutation supply is only the first filter. Chapter 4 distinguished events from descendant counts. Here a mutation's descendant count just before transfer also determines its chance of surviving the bottleneck. Model the transfer as independent inclusion of each cell with probability $q$. If a variant has $m$ cells at transfer, none are retained with probability $(1-q)^m$, so

\[ \Pr(\hbox{at least one retained})=1-(1-q)^m. \]

The model describes random thinning from a well-mixed population; sampling an exactly fixed number without replacement instead uses a hypergeometric probability. For $q=0.001$, a singleton is retained with probability 0.001. A clone of 20 has probability about 0.019811, and a clone of 1000 about 0.632305. An advantage during growth does not exempt a rare lineage from transfer loss.

When $q\ll1$, $(1-q)^m\approx e^{-qm}$. The expected number transferred, $qm$, is therefore the scale controlling the transition from likely loss to likely retention. But surviving one transfer is not eventual establishment or fixation. Further growth, drift, mutation and competition intervene. Increasing culture volume can alter both event supply and the sizes of the lineages presented to the same volume-fraction bottleneck.

Batch adaptation responds to the whole repeating environment Lecture 47, 17:45

The externally repeated recipe does not make the instantaneous cell environment constant. Resources are consumed, density changes, byproducts accumulate, and oxygen can vary during a batch. Cells experience a repeating sequence of lag, growth, depletion and stationary conditions. In the simplest density-independent comparison, two genotypes with cycle multiplication factors $A_1$ and $A_2$ change their odds by the factor $A_1/A_2$ before an unbiased transfer. The common dilution cancels from the ratio of expected counts, or from deterministic mean-count odds. It does not generally cancel from the expectation of the random count ratio; that ratio is undefined when its denominator lineage is lost. Sampling still determines the random loss of rare lineages.

If the instantaneous net rates were $g_1(t)$ and $g_2(t)$ in a prescribed common environment, the log-odds change would be $\int_0^{\Delta t}[g_1(t)-g_2(t)]\,dt$. This integral can favor a strain with a shorter lag or better survival even if it has a lower maximum exponential growth rate. Frequency dependence, resource competition and genotype-dependent environmental modification complicate the formula; they make the need to specify the assay stronger, rather than weaker.

Parallel lines permit the investigator to compare repeated histories. A line is an independently propagated population, not another sample tube from the same culture. Shared starting genotype and protocol constrain the comparison, while random mutation order and lineage fate can produce divergence. Sequence recurrence in a gene, recurrence of the exact substitution, and convergence of measured phenotype are distinct levels of repeatability. One endpoint isolate per line also need not represent all coexisting variation.

Serial transfers are convenient to replicate and archive, but logistical ease does not make their environment equivalent to uninterrupted nutrient limitation. The lecture's claim about a very large percentage of laboratory experiments using this platform is a qualitative motivation here; no surveyed numerical prevalence is asserted. The scientific choice should follow the question and the controlled variables.

A chemostat controls flow; steady state still requires biological balance Lecture 48, 6:15

A chemostat feeds sterile medium into a well-mixed vessel and removes an equal volume of culture. Let $V$ be constant vessel volume and $Q$ the volume flow per time. The dilution rate is $\delta=Q/V$, with units of inverse time. If $B(t)$ is cell density and $g(S)$ is the per-capita net growth rate at resource concentration $S$, the simplest biomass balance is

\[ \frac{dB}{dt}=[g(S)-\delta]B. \]

The loss term follows directly: the outflow carries $QB$ cells per time, and dividing by $V$ gives density loss $\delta B$. In a nonzero steady state, $g(S^*)=\delta$. Resource supply and consumption determine the corresponding $S^*$ and biomass. Continuous flow alone does not guarantee steady state. After an evolutionary change, altered uptake or yield can change the internal state even when the pumps and feed composition are unchanged.

This distinction is experimentally visible. Gresham and colleagues maintained nutrient-limited yeast populations at a stated dilution rate and assayed daily culture properties. Their glucose-limited populations changed residual glucose during evolution. Thus constant control settings and an approximately steady growth regime do not mean every measured internal chemical concentration remains permanently identical. See Gresham et al. (2008), Physiological phenotypes and Chemostat Culture.

For a nonreacting tracer in the constant-volume, well-mixed vessel, the probability of remaining in the reactor for at least time $t$ is $e^{-\delta t}$. Equivalently, a tracer pulse decays as $c(t)=c(0)e^{-\delta t}$. Integrating the survival function gives the mean residence time

\[ \mathbb E[T_{\mathrm{res}}]=\int_0^\infty e^{-\delta t}\,dt =\frac1\delta=\frac VQ. \]

A residence time is an average over an exponential distribution, not a deadline at which every original molecule exits. After one mean residence time, $e^{-1}\approx36.8\%$ of an ideal tracer pulse remains. Stopping inlet flow while continuing outflow changes vessel volume, so that experiment would require a different mass balance; it should not be inferred from the constant-volume exponential model.

If $\delta$ exceeds the maximum achievable net growth rate under the feed conditions, growth cannot replenish outflow and washout occurs. A maximum doubling time convention must be converted into a net rate: a minimum doubling time $\tau_{\min}$ corresponds to $g_{\max}=\ln2/\tau_{\min}$ in the simple exponential model. It does not correspond to $1/\tau_{\min}$. For illustrative $\tau_{\min}=20$ minutes, $g_{\max}\approx0.03466$ per minute, whereas a five-minute residence time implies $\delta=0.2$ per minute, far too high under that assumption. Twenty minutes is not a universal limit for all organisms and conditions.

The investigator gains controlled continuous nutrient limitation but also inherits pump stability, sterility, sampling and biofilm concerns. Natural habitats exchange matter, yet they are not thereby ideal well-mixed, constant-volume chemostats: flow intermittency and spatial structure can dominate. The apparatus isolates a useful limit, not a literal representation of every ecological niche.

Mutation accumulation reduces selective filtering without abolishing it Lecture 48, 19:51

Adaptive evolution experiments reveal mutations that survive drift and selection, including neutral hitchhikers linked to beneficial changes. They do not show only beneficial mutations, nor do they automatically estimate the full distribution of effects of all new mutations. If the investigator instead wants a less selectively filtered spectrum, severe recurrent bottlenecks can allow more mildly deleterious mutations to persist by chance.

In a mutation-accumulation experiment, separate lines repeatedly pass through a very small founder number. One implementation grows colonies from isolated cells and chooses a colony by a rule designed to avoid favoring visible size or another phenotype; descendants seed the next passage. Randomizing the transmitted lineage weakens between-lineage competition. It does not erase selection within a growing colony, allow lethal mutants to reproduce, or guarantee equal recovery of slow colonies. The lecture's “only drift, no selection” is an idealization that requires this correction.

A bottleneck also does not make census size one throughout the passage. Growth generates many divisions, and the sequenced final lineage samples only part of that genealogical history. The mutation exposure for an inherited endpoint change is counted along the transmitted lineage in generations, rather than by adding all births in every colony. Colony genealogy, deaths, transfer procedures and callable genomic regions affect the estimate.

Let line $j$ have $k_j$ detected retained mutations, $G_j$ estimated lineage generations and $L_j$ callable nucleotide positions, meaning positions where the sequencing and analysis can reliably detect the counted class of change. Under an original independent Poisson model with constant per-site rate $u$, its expected count is $uL_jG_j$. Maximizing the Poisson likelihood over lines gives

\[ \widehat u=\frac{\sum_j k_j}{\sum_j L_jG_j}. \]

The denominator has units of nucleotide-generations. If a complete common genome of length $L$ is callable, its genome-wide mean is estimated by $\widehat U=L\widehat u$. Incomplete callability cannot be repaired by casually multiplying a partial count by full genome size without assumptions about unobserved regions. Detection errors, pre-existing variants and selection bias also violate the simple likelihood.

Lee and colleagues' E. coli experiment used daily single-colony passages, estimated generations and whole-genome analysis to infer retained mutation rates. It supplies an empirical implementation, with explicit strain, mutation-class and filtering conventions, rather than evidence that every new mutation was observable. See Lee et al. (2012), Materials and Methods and Spontaneous Mutation Rate. To infer a distribution of fitness effects, the investigator must additionally measure effects in declared assays; an endpoint genome containing several mutations does not by itself identify each mutation's individual contribution or its epistasis with the others.

Resistance thresholds and reproductive performance are separate measurements Lecture 49, 2:20

The minimum inhibitory concentration, or MIC, is the lowest tested antibiotic concentration that prevents the defined visible-growth endpoint in a specified assay. It depends on medium, inoculum, incubation and reading procedure; this assay definition follows EUCAST's broth-dilution methods paper (2003), Introduction. Inhibition is not necessarily killing. MIC is also distinct from a clinical breakpoint used to classify susceptibility under a particular drug-organism treatment convention. Here MIC is a laboratory phenotype, and the lecture's language about “clinical level” is not used as its definition.

A schematic threshold picture makes the selection argument intuitive: below a strain's inhibition threshold, growth may occur; above it, the assay reports inhibition. Actual dose-response curves and discrete tested dilutions make a perfectly sharp continuous cutoff an approximation. An MIC increase establishes improved growth tolerance under that assay, not automatically faster growth at every subthreshold concentration.

Consider an original illustration with strains $A,B,C$ whose MICs are 1, 4 and 16 in arbitrary concentration units. At concentration 8, only $C$ is below its inhibition threshold. At concentration 2, both $B$ and $C$ can potentially grow. To decide their competition, measure their reproduction in that environment. If $B$ has the larger net multiplication factor, it is favored even though $C$ has the larger MIC. No principle requires this ordering: resistance costs can be absent, environment-dependent, or compensated by later mutations.

The investigator must therefore record resistance and growth performance separately. An evolutionary trade-off is a reduction in performance in one condition associated with improvement in another, not a necessary resource-allocation story inferred from MIC alone. Functional mechanisms, reconstruction of mutations, and matched assays distinguish causation from correlation.

The MEGA-plate makes intermediate environments spatially accessible Lecture 49, 10:37

A well-mixed drug concentration presents one challenge at a time. A spatial gradient permits populations to persist in less challenging regions while descendants enter harder ones. The lecture sketches a large semisolid plate with rising antibiotic bands. Motile bacteria spread through the permissive region; a lineage able to reproduce in the next region can expand there and supply further mutations. Chemotaxis, directed movement in response to a chemical gradient, contributes to resource-directed spread.

The primary measurements need their actual scale. Baym et al.'s 120-by-60-cm MEGA-plate used a soft motility layer over drug-containing sections. Their published trimethoprim bands were 0, 3, 30, 300 and 3000 times the wild-type MIC, rather than the lecture's schematic 0, 1, 10, 100 and 1000. Sampled high-region isolates showed MIC increases of $10^4$ for trimethoprim and $10^5$ for ciprofloxacin. A plate's local drug concentration and an isolate's separately measured MIC are different observations. See Baym et al. (2016), pp. 1148–1150, Figures 1–4.

The mathematical hinge is intermediate-lineage expansion. Suppose, as a toy model, a first-step genotype has $m$ reproductive opportunities in a permissive region, each with probability $p$ of generating a relevant next-step variant. Its chance of at least one event is $1-(1-p)^m$. Expansion increases $m$ and thus access to the next change. A genotype unable to reproduce in an abrupt high-drug environment may have very few opportunities there. This conditional argument does not prove that a specified set of mutations is literally impossible in one reproductive event: rare multiple events, standing variation and DNA transfer are other possible mechanisms.

For the same reason, failure to cross an abrupt barrier during a finite experiment means no successful crossing was observed under that design. It does not establish that every possible high-resistance genotype is genetically inaccessible, or that cells persisting in the low-drug region all went extinct. The lecture's dramatic direct-jump contrast is valuable when interpreted as evidence about accessible population trajectories and the time window, rather than as a universal impossibility theorem.

Nor is a paused visible front a stopwatch for the first mutation's birth. A variant can originate before it is large enough to observe, away from the sampled location, or before its descendants cross the boundary. Mutation, expansion, migration and detection all contribute to the waiting time. The original investigator should combine time-lapse observations with isolate phenotypes and genomic relationships, and distinguish an imputed ancestry from directly observed molecular mutation.

Front position changes which variants can shape the future Lecture 49, 23:31

Gene sequencing supplies another level of evidence. Repeated independent changes in a gene suggest a recurrent evolutionary target, but several sampled descendants can inherit the same original event. Counts of mutated isolates must therefore be distinguished from reconstructed independent events. The lecture highlights folA; this gene encodes dihydrofolate reductase, trimethoprim's molecular target. Mutations in stress-response or regulatory genes can provide other routes. Recurrence motivates causal tests rather than proving that every detected change increases resistance.

In the actual paper, gene-event counts used reconstructed ancestry, and resistance-associated changes often had growth costs that could later be compensated. Some compensated mutants behind the front had greater resistance than the advancing lineage; moving isolates forward demonstrated growth where the front could not advance. Thus spatial access could prevent a highly resistant lineage from directing the expansion. These observations are the specific primary-source extension beyond the lecture's simpler “fittest lineage wins” picture.

For our investigator, that extension changes the comparison with liquid culture. Local competitors and access to unoccupied resources can determine expansion; a globally superior assay score cannot move a lineage through occupied space. Front speed also reflects movement and resource gradients, so it is not automatically the same quantity as competitive reproductive fitness in a shaken flask. Even a successful spatial transplant requires controls for inoculum, motility and local resource state.

The apparatus reveals how intermediate challenge, population expansion and location interact. It is not a direct patient-treatment model. Changing plate dimensions, organismal motility, drug diffusion or inoculation geometry can change the opportunities it creates. The relevant transfer of insight is the explicit mechanism, accompanied by these experimental boundaries.

Calculate cycle balance and bottleneck capture

The following standard-library program implements two already explained calculations: expansion divided by dilution, and retention of at least one member of a clone under independent thinning. The function accepts an integer clone size from zero through $10^{12}$ and a finite transferred fraction in $[0,1]$; invalid inputs raise an exception. It returns a probability, not a fixation prediction. Logarithms and expm1 preserve accuracy when the capture probability is small. The fixed examples read no files, make no random draws, and produce no external effects.

import math


def capture_probability(mutants, fraction):
    if type(mutants) is not int or not 0 <= mutants <= 10 ** 12:
        raise ValueError("mutants must be an integer from 0 to 10**12")
    if not 0.0 <= fraction <= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("fraction must lie in [0, 1]")
    if mutants == 0 or fraction == 0:
        return 0.0
    if fraction == 1:
        return 1.0
    return -math.expm1(mutants * math.log1p(-fraction))


for expansion in (600, 1000, 1200):
    print(f"expansion={expansion} "
          f"cycle multiplier={expansion / 1000:.3f}")
for mutants in (1, 20, 1000):
    probability = capture_probability(mutants, 0.001)
    print(f"mutants={mutants} capture={probability:.6f}")

The deterministic cycle map and the random clone-retention model address different stages. Keeping mean population size stable does not guarantee retaining a particular rare variant. Conversely, retaining a clone once does not ensure the population's long-run demographic persistence. To infer both from data, the investigator needs measurements of batch expansion and the bottleneck protocol.

Exercises

1. Choose a growth interval that can compensate dilution

In the constant-rate approximation, take $g=0.4$ per hour, lag $\ell=2$ hours and dilution $d=1000$. Find the minimum interval that compensates dilution. If actual expansion is only 600 per cycle, compute the expected fraction remaining after ten cycles and explain a limitation.

Worked solution

The equality $g(\Delta t-\ell)=\ln1000$ gives $\Delta t=2+\ln1000/0.4\approx19.2694$ hours. It presumes nutrients permit the required expansion and that the stated rate remains applicable. With actual expansion 600, the cycle multiplier is 0.6 and after ten cycles the fraction is $0.6^{10}\approx0.00604662$. The deterministic calculation tracks an expected count; at low counts, transfer extinction is a discrete stochastic event, so one cannot interpret the formula as a certain surviving fractional population.

2. Compare lineage retention in different culture volumes

Two well-mixed cultures have final counts $10^{10}$ and $10^{11}$. In each, a variant has frequency $10^{-8}$, and the transferred fraction is $q=0.001$. Calculate clone sizes and capture probabilities. Compare expected event supplies with a common illustrative $U=10^{-3}$ and complete regrowth.

Worked solution

The variant has100 and1000 cells respectively. Under independent thinning, capture probabilities are $1-(0.999)^{100}\approx0.0952079$ and $1-(0.999)^{1000}\approx0.632305$. Identical frequencies do not imply identical bottleneck survival because absolute counts matter. The smaller culture has $10^{10}(1-0.001)=9.99\times10^9$ divisions and expected $9.99\times10^6$ mutation events; the larger has ten times each. These conclusions assume equal rate and physiological conditions. Capture and mutation supply are separate probabilities and counts, neither of which alone identifies eventual fixation.

3. Audit a residence-time claim and a washout design

A 100-liter well-mixed vessel has equal inlet/outlet flow of 20 liters per minute. Find dilution rate, mean residence time and remaining fraction of a nonreacting tracer after five minutes. Under an illustrative minimum cell doubling time of 20 minutes, can a nonzero steady biomass be maintained by the simple growth model?

Worked solution

The dilution rate is $\delta=20/100=0.2$ per minute and mean residence time is $1/\delta=5$ minutes. The tracer fraction after5 minutes is $e^{-0.2(5)}=e^{-1}\approx0.367879$, not zero. The maximal net growth rate under the stated approximation is $\ln2/20\approx0.0346574$ per minute. Since0.2 exceeds that rate, no positive biomass can balance removal; $dB/dt<0$ even at maximal growth. The conclusion uses the supplied growth bound, not a universal assertion about bacterial physiology, and presumes constant volume, uniform mixing and no attached reservoir of cells.

4. Estimate mutation rate with the correct genealogical denominator

Fifty accumulation lines each undergo200 passages estimated at28 transmitted-lineage generations per passage. Each has4million callable positions, and600 counted retained substitutions are detected in total. Estimate the per-position and callable-genome means. Why not divide by all divisions in the colonies, and why not call this the unfiltered mutation spectrum?

Worked solution

The lineage exposure is $50(200)(28)=280{,}000$ generations across lines. Multiplying by $4\times10^6$ callable positions gives $1.12\times10^{12}$ nucleotide-generations. Thus $\widehat u=600/(1.12\times10^{12})\approx5.35714\times10^{-10}$ substitutions per position per generation, and the mean over the callable region is $4\times10^6\widehat u\approx0.00214286$ per generation. Endpoint changes lie in the ancestry of the transmitted lineage, not in every discarded cell lineage, so total colony births would use the wrong denominator for this observation. Mutations preventing colony formation are missing; within-colony selection and variant detection still matter. The estimate concerns the declared retained and detectable class.

5. Infer competitive performance from a threshold assay cautiously

Strains B and C have MICs of 4 and 16. At concentration 2, their net rates measured in the same prescribed environment are 0.8 and 0.6 per hour. Starting at equal counts and ignoring density dependence, compute their ratio after five hours. At concentration 8, what can the MIC observation establish without a killing assay?

Worked solution

At concentration 2, $N_B/N_C=e^{(0.8-0.6)5}=e\approx2.71828$, so B increases in relative abundance despite its lower MIC. The calculation uses actual stated rate measurements, not an assumed universal resistance cost. At concentration 8, the endpoint assay inhibits visible growth of B under its specified conditions and permits potential growth of C. It does not establish that B is killed, that every B cell dies, or that a clinical treatment is effective. Growth curves, viable-cell measurements and the relevant treatment convention would answer different questions.

6. Test whether a lineage is trapped by space or incapable of expansion

An isolate behind an advancing front has a higher MIC than a front isolate. Design a comparison that tests spatial limitation, and explain what genome-event reconstruction adds to the images.

Worked solution

Recover both isolates and assay their MICs, growth and motility under matched conditions. Inoculate equal viable counts into the same fresh region ahead of the original front, with matched medium/drug concentration, independent replicates and handling controls. If the rear isolate expands there while the front isolate cannot, absence from the original front is consistent with constrained spatial access rather than intrinsic inability in that region. Side-by-side fresh gradients test competition under a second declared geometry; neither assay proves universal superiority. Time-lapse lineage boundaries and genome relationships help distinguish one inherited mutation seen in several descendants from independent recurrent events. They constrain ancestry but do not directly photograph a nucleotide change at its birth. Reconstruction and functional tests are necessary before treating each colored patch or repeated sequence call as a separate adaptive event.
