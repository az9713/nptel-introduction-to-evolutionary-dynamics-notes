Continuous culture and selection relative to the mean

Lectures 18–20 · Flow balances, frequency dynamics, and mutation effects

A resistant mutant increases from 1% to 7% of a mixed culture while the total population falls by more than half. Has the mutant succeeded? Its relative representation has increased, but this says neither that the vessel maintains a constant number of cells nor that resistance is advantageous in another environment. A continuous-culture model must account separately for fluid volume, total abundance, and genotype frequency.

Our original running experiment has a one-litre, well-mixed vessel, a sterile inflow of 0.5 litres per hour, and an equal outflow. In a short interval over which we approximate growth rates as constant, residents A grow at \(g_A=0.4\) per hour and mutants B at \(g_B=0.6\) per hour. The initial count is 10,000 and the mutant fraction is 0.01. These deliberately small synthetic numbers are not an experimental estimate. They expose what the lecture’s mean-growth argument proves, and what requires an additional physical assumption.

1. Equal flows fix volume, not population size L18 · 2:17

A chemostat supplies fresh medium continuously and removes culture liquid continuously. A well-mixed reactor approximates the concentration of each constituent as spatially uniform; this is a statement about location, not about constancy over time. Its outflow has the same concentration as the liquid remaining in the vessel. Sterile inflow contains nutrients but no cells in our model. Cell attachment to the walls, settling, immigration, and spatial gradients are excluded.

Let \(V\) be liquid volume, \(F\) the equal inlet and outlet volumetric flow, and \(D=F/V\) the dilution rate, in inverse hours. The volume balance is \(\dot V=F-F=0\). If A cells are uniformly mixed, their concentration is \(A/V\), so their outflow loss is \(F(A/V)=DA\) cells per hour. The same per-cell removal rate applies to B. Thus, with constant per-capita net growth rates before flow removal,

\[ \dot A=(g_A-D)A,\qquad \dot B=(g_B-D)B. \tag{6.1} \]

The word “net” permits intrinsic death to have been included in \(g_A,g_B\), but flow loss is subtracted separately. This chapter’s growth-rate-ratio convention later requires a positive resident rate. A genotype with zero or negative net rate can still be compared through rate differences, even though that ratio is unsuitable.

Define total count \(N=A+B\), mutant fraction \(x=B/N\) when \(N>0\), and population mean growth rate \(\bar g=(1-x)g_A+xg_B\). Adding (6.1) gives

\[ \dot N=(\bar g-D)N. \tag{6.2} \]

A positive steady population requires \(\bar g=D\). Equal flows alone do not enforce this condition. In the running case, the initial mean is \(0.99(0.4)+0.01(0.6)=0.402\) per hour, below \(D=0.5\), so the count initially declines even though the mutant individually grows faster than it is removed.

The lecture treats a constant total population and writes a common removal term \(\phi\), then infers that \(\phi\) equals the population mean. That is a consistent normalisation or a feedback-controlled replacement model. In a physical chemostat with prescribed \(F,V\), however, removal is already \(D=F/V\); it cannot independently follow a changing mean unless flow or actual resource-dependent growth changes. We will retain the valid frequency result by deriving it from (6.1), without assuming that the total count is constant. A chemostat operating at one steady state can leave it when a new genotype changes the population.

2. Frequency changes relative to a population mean L18 · 18:24 L19 · 2:31

Differentiate the quotient \(x=B/N\). The change in its denominator is essential:

\[ \dot x=\frac{\dot B}{N}-x\frac{\dot N}{N} =(g_B-D)x-(\bar g-D)x =(g_B-\bar g)x. \tag{6.3} \]

The common removal cancels. This equation governs frequency even when both genotypes decline in absolute abundance. Substituting the two-type mean gives the familiar replicator equation, a frequency equation in which a type’s relative growth is its rate minus the population mean:

\[ \dot x=\Delta g\,x(1-x),\qquad \Delta g=g_B-g_A. \tag{6.4} \]

The lecture calls its A fraction \(X\) and its B fraction \(Y\). Our book keeps \(x\) for mutant B, so \(Y=x\) and \(X=1-x\). Both conventions sum to one. A fraction can equal zero, so strict positivity is assumed only for an interior comparison, not for every population.

The mean is a convex combination: it lies between \(g_A\) and \(g_B\). For \(g_B>g_A\) and \(0<x<1\), B remains above the mean and A below it throughout the constant-rate two-type trajectory. The faster type cannot later fall below that mean and reverse direction merely because it becomes common. A reversal requires changing rates, additional types, or another omitted process.

The same reasoning extends to \(m\) types with frequencies \(p_j\), constant rates \(g_j\), and common dilution. Here \(\sum_jp_j=1\) and \(\bar g=\sum_jp_jg_j\); quotient differentiation gives \(\dot p_j=(g_j-\bar g)p_j\). A type above the mean increases in frequency. Its absolute count increases only when \(g_j>D\). An intermediate-rate type can first increase and later decline in frequency as the mean passes its rate. This is possible with three types even while every \(g_j\) stays constant.

For constant rates, the mean’s derivative has a particularly useful form:

\[ \frac{d\bar g}{dt} =\sum_j g_j(g_j-\bar g)p_j =\sum_jp_j(g_j-\bar g)^2\ge0. \tag{6.5} \]

The last expression is the frequency-weighted variance of growth rates. The equality follows by expanding the square and using \(\sum_jp_j=1\). This is a precise result for the stated model, not a universal assertion that evolutionary fitness always increases. If growth rates depend explicitly on time, resource levels, or frequencies, differentiating the mean adds \(\sum_jp_j\dot g_j\), which can be negative.

3. Exact trajectories and time to a specified frequency L19 · 8:49

For an interior frequency, divide (6.4) by \(x(1-x)\). Since the derivative of \(\ln[x/(1-x)]\) with respect to \(x\) is \(1/[x(1-x)]\), integration gives

\[ \ln\frac{x(t)}{1-x(t)} =\ln\frac{x_0}{1-x_0}+\Delta g\,t,\qquad x(t)=\frac{x_0e^{\Delta g t}}{1-x_0+x_0e^{\Delta g t}}. \tag{6.6} \]

For \(\Delta g=0\), the frequency stays at \(x_0\). For a positive difference and an interior initial frequency, it approaches one as \(t\) tends to infinity but does not equal one at finite time. A deterministic fractional abundance below one cell warns that the continuous approximation is no longer describing individual-cell outcomes. Finite-population loss and fixation require a stochastic model, developed later in the book.

To reach an interior target \(x_1>x_0\) when \(\Delta g>0\), the required time is

\[ t_1=\frac{1}{\Delta g} \ln\frac{x_1(1-x_0)}{x_0(1-x_1)}. \tag{6.7} \]

The log argument is a ratio of odds, and the units come entirely from \(1/\Delta g\). In the running construction, increasing from 0.01 to 0.5 takes \(\ln99/0.2=22.975599\) hours. Reaching 0.9 requires \(\ln891/0.2=33.96172\) hours. Taking the target to one sends the predicted time to infinity.

Absolute counts follow directly from (6.1): \(A(t)=9900e^{-0.1t}\) and \(B(t)=100e^{0.1t}\). At 10 hours, the total has declined to 3913.835 while B’s fraction has risen to 0.069453. The total’s minimum occurs when \(\bar g=D\), which here means \(x=0.5\); its count is \(2\sqrt{9900(100)}=1989.975\). Thereafter the constant-rate model predicts renewed unlimited total growth. That extrapolation identifies the need for resource feedback; it is not a realistic long-term chemostat prediction.

Unlike the batch shared-capacity model, the constant-rate frequency equation has no factor that vanishes as a finite resource is exhausted. Its selection exposure continues indefinitely. Both models can describe increasing mutant frequency, but their assumptions about how growth opportunity ends differ. Matching the environment is therefore necessary before reusing an equation.

4. A physical resource balance supplies the missing feedback

To make the limitation explicit, consider a separate deterministic teaching extension with one strain. Let \(M\) be biomass concentration in milligrams per litre, \(S\) limiting-substrate concentration in the same mass-per-volume units, \(S_{\mathrm{in}}\) its feed concentration, and \(Y>0\) the biomass mass produced per substrate mass consumed. This extension tracks biomass rather than literal cell count, avoiding an assumption that every cell always has the same mass. Its well-mixed balances are

\[ \dot M=[\mu(S)-D]M,\qquad \dot S=D(S_{\mathrm{in}}-S)-\frac{\mu(S)M}{Y}. \tag{6.8} \]

The first term in the substrate equation is net feed supply; the second converts biomass production into substrate consumption. We omit maintenance consumption, cell death, multiple resources, and variable yield. A useful saturating rate law is \(\mu(S)=\mu_{\max}S/(H+S)\), with positive maximum rate \(\mu_{\max}\) and half-saturation concentration \(H\). At \(S=H\), growth reaches half the maximum; this is a stipulated kinetic model, not a fit to our fictitious strains.

These balances and this rate law are also set out in the primary modelling paper by Fritsch, Harmand, and Campillo, §2.1, equations (1)–(3). Their yield notation \(k\) is our \(1/Y\), and their bacterial biomass variable is our \(M\). The paper distinguishes deterministic population descriptions from individual stochastic descriptions. We use only the stated two-variable balance here. Fritsch et al., arXiv:1405.7963, §2.1.

At a positive equilibrium, the biomass equation requires \(\mu(S_*)=D\). Solving the specified rate law gives

\[ S_*=\frac{HD}{\mu_{\max}-D},\qquad M_*=Y(S_{\mathrm{in}}-S_*). \tag{6.9} \]

The solution requires \(D<\mu_{\max}\) and \(S_*<S_{\mathrm{in}}\), equivalently \(D<\mu(S_{\mathrm{in}})\). Otherwise this model has no positive equilibrium compatible with the feed. The washout equilibrium is \(M=0,S=S_{\mathrm{in}}\); small biomass near it has growth exponent \(\mu(S_{\mathrm{in}})-D\). Negative exponent means local decline to washout. Equality is a threshold requiring more than the sign of the linear term.

A steady state can thus balance growth and removal through a lowered substrate concentration. It does not fill a geometrically predetermined number of cell “slots.” For two strains consuming the same substrate, growth functions matter at the residual concentration. As a synthetic counterexample to choosing the largest maximum rate, take \(\mu_A(S)=0.6S/(0.1+S)\), \(\mu_B(S)=0.8S/(0.3+S)\), and \(D=0.2\) per hour. A alone leaves \(S_A^*=0.05\) milligrams per litre; B alone would require \(S_B^*=0.10\). At A’s equilibrium, a rare B has exponent \(0.8(0.05)/(0.35)-0.2=-0.085714\) per hour. B has the larger maximum rate but cannot invade that equilibrium under these assumptions.

This calculation proves a local invasion result for the specified single-resource model. It does not establish a universal competitive-exclusion rule for cultures with cross-feeding, attachment, several resources, or changing environments. It also explains why “faster-growing” must specify the substrate concentration at which growth was measured.

5. A mutation’s effect has a denominator and a context L19 · 15:21 L20 · 0:34

The preceding equations follow existing types whose descendants retain their type. They do not create a third genotype. A mutation, a heritable sequence change, can change a measured growth rate or leave it unchanged. Relative to a declared ancestor in a declared assay, an increase is beneficial, a decrease deleterious, and no change neutral with respect to that fitness measure. Neutral is not the absence of a DNA change and does not imply the absence of effects on every other phenotype.

Let the positive reference fitness measure be \(f_A\) and the comparable mutant measure \(f_B\), in the same units. The lectures define relative fitness and the dimensionless selection coefficient by

\[ r=\frac{f_B}{f_A},\qquad s=r-1=\frac{f_B-f_A}{f_A}. \tag{6.10} \]

If fitness here means positive constant growth rate, set \(f_A=g_A,f_B=g_B\). Then \(\Delta g=g_As\), not \(s\) itself, is the exponent per hour in (6.6). A mutant growing at 1.05 per hour against a resident at 1 per hour has \(s=0.05\), whereas another at 1.03 has \(s=0.03\). Both are beneficial under this assay, but their time scales differ. With \(g_A=0.4,g_B=0.6\), \(s=0.5\) and \(\Delta g=0.2\) per hour.

A log growth-rate ratio \(\ln r\) is dimensionless and differs from \(s\). A finite-interval log-fold advantage is \(\ell=\Delta g\,T\) under constant rates, also dimensionless but duration dependent. It is not generally \(\ln r\). Setting an equation’s exponent to “selection coefficient” without specifying these conventions can introduce an arbitrary time unit into the predicted trajectory.

Lecture 20 stresses two kinds of context. A mutation can depend on the genetic background, the other sequence variants carried by the organism. It can also depend on the environment in which the assay is performed. The lactose-transporter illustration proposes a mutation useful when lactose uptake limits growth and irrelevant in another carbon-source condition. Irrelevance is an assumption to test: altered expression, energetic costs, or other functions could invalidate it. The resistance illustration proposes a mutant advantaged in a drug environment but disadvantaged without the drug. It is an example of a possible trade-off, not a theorem that every resistance mutation has a cost.

A concise way to preserve both dependencies is to write \(s(m\mid G,E,\mathcal A)\): the effect of mutation \(m\) in background \(G\), environment \(E\), and assay \(\mathcal A\). A single nucleotide replacement does not carry an environment-independent beneficial label. Likewise, all exactly neutral mutations share \(s=0\) under one chosen measure, but need not share molecular mechanism or fitness in another condition.

Finally, the deterministic prediction that a slower type declines in frequency is not a proof that every deleterious lineage certainly becomes extinct. Even (6.1) retains positive real counts at finite time from positive initial conditions. Finite populations permit stochastic outcomes, and changing environments or continued mutation introduce still other possibilities. The next chapter studies variation in effects across mutations before later chapters study their random fates.

6. Executable balances and checks

The standard-library companion chapter06_chemostat.py evaluates the constant-rate counts and computes the positive break-even substrate root. It rejects nonfinite inputs, invalid frequencies, and rates outside the declared domains. These examples have moderate exponential arguments; unrestricted long-time extrapolation can overflow and is biologically inappropriate. Run this listing in the companion file’s directory:

from math import log
from chapter06_chemostat import competition, break_even

for time in (0.0, 10.0, log(99) / 0.2):
    a, b, x, mean = competition(time)
    print(f"t={time:9.6f} h: total={a + b:10.3f}, "
          f"x={x:.6f}, mean={mean:.6f}/h")
sa = break_even(0.6, 0.1, 0.2)
sb = break_even(0.8, 0.3, 0.2)
invasion = 0.8 * sa / (0.3 + sa) - 0.2
print(f"S*_A={sa:.6f}, S*_B={sb:.6f} mg/L")
print(f"B invasion exponent at A equilibrium={invasion:.6f}/h")

7. Worked exercises

Exercise 1. The flow coefficient is known

A two-litre reactor has equal inlet and outlet flows of 0.4 litres per hour. A genotype has \(10^8\) cells per litre and a growth rate of 0.15 per hour. Compute its removal flux, net count change, and whether its frequency must decline.

Solution

The count is \(2\times10^8\), dilution is \(D=0.2\) per hour, and removal is \(0.4(10^8)=4\times10^7\) cells per hour. Growth adds \(3\times10^7\), so net count change is \(-10^7\) cells per hour. Its frequency depends on the other types’ rates: it can increase in frequency if the mean is below 0.15 even while its own count declines. No unknown proportionality constant is needed to calculate the outflow under the well-mixed assumption.

Exercise 2. Frequency targets and extinction

For \(\Delta g=0.2\) per hour, calculate the time to rise from 1% to 90%. Explain why “time to eliminate the resident” is not obtained by substituting \(x_1=1\) into the same finite formula.

Solution

The odds ratio changes from \(1/99\) to 9, a factor of 891. Equation (6.7) gives \(\ln891/0.2=33.96172\) hours. As \(x_1\) tends to one, the target odds diverge and the time tends to infinity. A real count becomes discrete near extinction, so the deterministic real-valued curve cannot supply an individual-level extinction time by rounding its endpoint. A stochastic model must define the event and its time convention.

Exercise 3. An intermediate type changes direction

Three types have rates \(0.2,0.4,0.8\) per hour and equal starting frequencies. Which frequencies initially rise? Which types can reverse direction while their rates stay constant?

Solution

The initial mean is \(1.4/3=0.466667\), so only the 0.8 type rises initially; both others decline. To show an intermediate type rising and then falling, change the initial composition so the mean begins below 0.4, for example \((0.8,0.19,0.01)\), with mean 0.244. The 0.4 type then rises initially. The 0.8 type is present and its relative odds against both slower types grow exponentially, so the mean approaches 0.8 and eventually exceeds 0.4. The intermediate type then declines. The fastest present type always remains above the interior mean and cannot reverse under constant rates; the slowest cannot rise. Equation (6.5) describes this monotone mean without requiring every frequency to be monotone.

Exercise 4. A resource-dependent winner

Use the two synthetic growth functions in §4. Compute the invasion exponent of rare A at B’s equilibrium. Explain the discrepancy between maximum-growth-rate ranking and invasion ranking.

Solution

B’s break-even concentration is 0.10 milligrams per litre. At that concentration A grows at \(0.6(0.10)/(0.20)=0.3\) per hour, giving invasion exponent \(0.3-0.2=0.1\) per hour. Conversely B’s exponent at A’s equilibrium is negative. A has a lower maximum rate but grows sufficiently well at a lower residual substrate concentration. The local calculation assumes the resident equilibrium exists, feed exceeds its residual requirement, and the invader does not initially alter the resource appreciably. It does not resolve cultures with multiple resources or other interactions.

Exercise 5. Translate selection conventions

A resident grows at 0.5 per hour and a mutant at 0.55 per hour. Compute \(s\), \(\Delta g\), \(\ln r\), and the four-hour log-fold advantage. Repeat the rate difference in inverse minutes and show that the predicted odds multiplier is unchanged.

Solution

The growth-rate ratio is 1.1, so \(s=0.1\), \(\Delta g=0.05\) per hour, and \(\ln r=0.0953102\). For four hours the log-fold advantage is \(\ell=0.2\), giving an odds multiplier \(e^{0.2}=1.22140\). In inverse minutes, \(\Delta g=0.05/60\); multiplying by 240 minutes again gives 0.2. Relative ratios and log ratios are unit invariant, but the dimensional rate difference must transform with the time coordinate. Substituting \(s=0.1\) as an exponent per hour would incorrectly double the predicted advantage.

Exercise 6. Alternating environments

In a no-lag, no-mutation construction, a resistance mutant has \(\Delta g=0.08\) per hour during drug exposure and \(\Delta g=-0.02\) per hour without the drug. A cycle contains \(T_d\) drug hours and \(T_0\) drug-free hours. Derive the cycle odds multiplier and the condition for a positive cycle advantage. State a reason this calculation may fail in a real switch.

Solution

Integrating the log-odds derivative gives \(\ell=0.08T_d-0.02T_0\), with odds multiplier \(e^\ell\). The mutant gains relative representation when \(T_d>T_0/4\). The order of intervals does not matter in this particular memoryless constant-rate model. Real switching can introduce genotype-dependent lag, death, drug persistence, or resource changes, making rates history dependent and order relevant. The assumed drug-free cost is part of this synthetic example; it does not establish a cost for every resistance mechanism or a prediction for treatment.
