Distributions, landscapes, and epistasis

Lectures 21–24 · What variation offers, and which paths it permits

A beneficial mutation raises a strain’s measured fitness. Must the next beneficial mutation still be beneficial after that first change? If the two mutations interact, their order can change the available routes. Before predicting an evolutionary trajectory, we need three separate objects: a distribution of mutational effects, a graph of possible sequence changes, and a fitness function on that graph.

Our running construction begins with three variable DNA positions in an otherwise fixed genome. In an additive score model, the sequence TAA can improve through GAA to GCA. We will prove when GCA is the unique endpoint, then replace the additive rule with a two-position interaction that has two different endpoints from the same start. All numerical landscapes in this chapter are declared teaching constructions. The qualitative lecture models are retained, while unresolved ASR table entries are not presented as exact recording values.

1. A distribution requires a sampling rule L21 · 0:31

A distribution of fitness effects, abbreviated DFE, specifies the probabilities of different mutation effects under a defined mutation-sampling procedure, genetic background, environment, and fitness assay. Following the previous chapter, take a positive ancestral fitness \(f_0\), comparable mutant fitness \(f_m\ge0\), and effect \(s_m=f_m/f_0-1\). The distribution concerns a randomly sampled mutation \(m\), not a randomly chosen organism after selection has acted.

The lecture’s conceptual experiment returns to the same ancestor for each newly introduced mutation. This matters. Introducing a succession of mutations into descendants instead would change the background, so their effects could be drawn from different distributions. Likewise, deliberately testing 100 beneficial and 100 deleterious mutations samples two conditional distributions; the resulting equal column sizes do not establish that half of naturally arising mutations are beneficial.

For a declared set of \(M\) mutations tested once each with equal weight, the empirical distribution assigns mass \(1/M\) to each measured \(s_m\). Its cumulative distribution function is \(\widehat P(s\le a)=M^{-1}\sum_m\mathbf1\{s_m\le a\}\). Here \(\mathbf1\) is an indicator, equal to one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. A histogram bins these values. A bin’s probability is its count divided by \(M\); a density estimate additionally divides by the bin width, so unequal-width bins cannot be compared through raw counts alone.

The three sign classes have probabilities \(P(s<0)\), \(P(s=0)\), and \(P(s>0)\). Exact neutrality can appear as a probability mass at zero; it need not be described by a continuous density. In a noisy assay, “indistinguishable from zero” instead depends on uncertainty and a detection threshold. It is evidence about resolution, not proof of an exactly zero biological effect.

For illustration, take ten synthetic effects: \(-0.20,-0.10,-0.05,-0.01,0,0,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.10\). Equal weighting gives deleterious, neutral, and beneficial fractions 0.4, 0.2, and 0.4. Conditional on being beneficial, the mean effect is 0.045. The mean across all ten is \(-0.018\). These are different questions with different denominators.

A biochemical library can weight genotypes uniformly, while spontaneous mutation weights changes by their actual mutation probabilities. A sample collected after growth can add differential survival, establishment, and expansion. If an effect-dependent detection probability is \(q(s)\), then the observed distribution is proportional to \(q(s)\) times the generated distribution. As a transparent toy rule for the four positive effects above, choose \(q(s)\propto s\). Their observed mean becomes \(\sum s^2/\sum s=0.072222\), larger than 0.045. This is an ascertainment model, not an estimate of fixation probabilities.

The lecture sketches many small beneficial effects and few large ones. An exponential conditional density \(p_+(s)=\beta^{-1}e^{-s/\beta}\) for \(s>0\), with mean \(\beta>0\), expresses that pattern, but it is not a universal DFE. In a primary experiment, MacLean and Buckling tested rifampicin-resistance mutants of Pseudomonas aeruginosa across drug concentrations. Their Results/Discussion and Table 2 report different support for an exponential effect distribution as conditions changed, and Methods describe how mutants were isolated. This selected mutant collection is not every possible genomic mutation. The relevant lesson is the dependence on assay and sampling, not a universal fitted shape. MacLean and Buckling, 2009.

2. Sequence space is a graph with a declared alphabet L21 · 21:10 L22 · 2:05

Fix an ordered DNA sequence length \(L\) and alphabet \(\mathcal B=\{A,T,G,C\}\). The sequence space is the set \(\mathcal B^L\), containing \(4^L\) strings because each position independently has four allowed choices. This is a combinatorial count, not a claim that all strings encode viable organisms. We fix orientation and length; insertions, deletions, rearrangements, and reverse-complement identifications require other conventions.

The Hamming distance \(h(a,b)\) between equal-length strings counts the positions at which they differ:

\[ h(a,b)=\sum_{j=1}^L\mathbf1\{a_j\ne b_j\}. \tag{7.1} \]

For example, AA and AT have distance one, while GGCCATT and GGGGATT have distance two. The latter comparison counts two changed positions, whatever a transcription’s spoken count might suggest. Distance zero means identical strings. Ordinary Hamming distance cannot directly compare unequal lengths; edit distances can, but define a different graph.

Join two vertices by an undirected edge when their distance is one. Every vertex has \(3L\) neighbours: select one of \(L\) positions and replace its letter by one of the other three. Distinct choices give distinct neighbours. Counting each edge at both endpoints gives

\[ |\mathcal V|=4^L,\qquad \deg(a)=3L,\qquad |\mathcal E|=\frac{4^L\,3L}{2}. \tag{7.2} \]

For \(L=2\), the graph has 16 vertices, degree six, and 48 edges. The neighbours of AA are AT, AG, AC, TA, GA, and CA. A drawing that places vertices in a plane is merely a layout of this graph; visual proximity on the page does not define mutational proximity. Nor does a three-dimensional mountain drawing faithfully preserve every adjacency of a large sequence space.

A useful generalisation counts the shell at Hamming distance \(k\). Choose the \(k\) changed positions and one of three alternatives at each, giving \(\binom Lk3^k\) vertices. Summing these shell sizes over \(k=0,\ldots,L\) gives \((1+3)^L=4^L\), a consistency check on the construction.

Experimental landscapes often vary only \(d\) selected positions with two selected alleles each. This gives a binary subgraph with \(2^d\) vertices and degree \(d\), rather than all \(4^L\) possible whole-genome strings. Every unvaried part of the genome and the assay environment must remain specified. A local peak in this restricted graph need not be a peak when further substitutions, loci, or environments are permitted.

3. Why one-step edges are useful, and what they omit L23 · 2:22

An edge represents one allowed substitution; it does not say how often that change occurs or whether its descendants succeed. Let \(U\) be the expected number of genome-wide substitution events per cell division, and \(u_{a\to b}\) the probability of a particular rare substitution producing neighbour \(b\) from \(a\) in that division. Those quantities have different denominators. Under a deliberately uniform substitution model, \(u_{a\to b}\approx U/(3L)\); actual mutation spectra can make the alternatives unequal.

If \(C\) divisions occur, the expected number of occurrences of the specified change is \(Cu_{a\to b}\), by summing indicators. This counts production, not established lineages. With independent small probabilities, a Poisson approximation gives \(P(\text{at least one})\approx1-e^{-Cu_{a\to b}}\). Merely having \(Cu\) near one does not guarantee an occurrence, let alone eventual replacement of the population.

The lecture contrasts a specific single change of order \(u=10^{-9}\) with two independent specified changes in one offspring, of order \(u^2=10^{-18}\). Used as illustrative probabilities, \(10^{10}\) divisions give about ten single-change occurrences but only \(10^{-8}\) simultaneous double occurrences. This separation explains the single-substitution approximation in that regime. It does not make a double change logically impossible. Correlated events, recombination, and larger exposure budgets can alter the comparison.

Two substitutions can also accumulate sequentially through an intermediate lineage. The intermediate need not fix before the second change appears. Requiring the whole population to occupy each intermediate is an additional weak-mutation approximation: successful mutant lineages are sufficiently separated in time that a sequence of substitutions describes the population. In a strong-selection uphill walk, only beneficial successful substitutions are retained. Those assumptions make the mountain analogy useful, but exclude coexistence of competing mutant lineages, neutral movement, and valley crossing. The graph itself supplies none of those dynamics.

4. Fitness attaches values to vertices L22 · 9:07

A fitness landscape is a graph of allowed genotypes together with an assay-dependent fitness function \(F:\mathcal V\to\mathbb R\). When relative effects use ratios, take the relevant \(F\) values positive; a zero value needs separate interpretation, such as no reproductive recovery under that assay. The abstract assignment does not require the impossible experiment of constructing every whole-genome sequence inside one organism.

The height metaphor distinguishes high and low values, but a population is usually a distribution over vertices, not one vertex. Its mean fitness can change as that distribution changes. The substitution-walk approximation represents it by one predominant genotype only under the additional assumptions just stated.

The lectures connect this metaphor to Sewall Wright’s 1932 essay. The primary reprint explicitly concerns biparental reproduction and combinations of genes; its discussion of peaks and population distributions is broader than a literal microbial nucleotide walk. We use the modern fixed-sequence graph without claiming that it was Wright’s exact molecular construction. Wright, 1932, reprint pp. 1 and 5.

A strict local peak is a vertex whose fitness exceeds every allowed neighbour’s. A global maximum exceeds or equals every vertex. Every strict global maximum is a strict local peak, but the converse fails. If ties are allowed, a plateau can have no strictly improving edge without being a strict peak; the walk must specify whether neutral moves are permitted.

Orient every unequal-fitness edge from lower to higher value. A strictly uphill path cannot revisit a vertex because its fitness rises at every step. On a finite graph it therefore terminates after finitely many steps at a vertex without a fitter neighbour. This graph statement says neither that every permitted beneficial mutation occurs nor that a real population has enough time to reach that endpoint.

5. What an additive landscape actually guarantees L23 · 16:47

A locus here is a specified sequence position. Suppose the measured score is the sum of independent position-specific contributions,

\[ F(a_1,\ldots,a_L)=c+\sum_{j=1}^L f_j(a_j). \tag{7.3} \]

Changing one letter changes only its own term. This is “no epistasis” on the declared additive score scale. Our consistent original three-position table is:

Original additive contributions in arbitrary score units
LetterPosition 1Position 2Position 3
A0.20.40.5
T0.10.20.1
G0.40.30.2
C0.30.60.3

With \(c=0\), TAA scores \(0.1+0.4+0.5=1.0\), GAA scores 1.3, and GCA scores 1.5. Each position has a unique best letter, respectively G, C, and A. Thus GCA maximises every term and is the unique global maximum.

More strongly, any other genotype differs from GCA at some position. Replacing that letter by its uniquely best alternative gives a single allowed edge with strictly higher score. Therefore no other genotype can be a local peak or an uphill terminal point. Every maximal strictly uphill path reaches GCA. The hinge is the existence of an improving single-position change from every nonoptimal genotype, not the visual smoothness of a drawn mountain.

The endpoint is unique; the path is not. TAA can also improve first to TCA and then to GCA. Other paths may pass through intermediate improvements that do not directly choose the best letter. If a position has tied best letters, several global maxima or a neutral plateau can exist even without epistasis. Additivity alone therefore does not imply a unique terminal sequence unless those ties are excluded.

6. Interactions have a scale and a sign L24 · 16:29

Epistasis means that a mutation’s effect depends on other genetic variants. Consider two binary loci, labelled 0 for the chosen ancestral allele and 1 for the chosen alternative. Write their four measured fitness values as \(F_{00},F_{10},F_{01},F_{11}\). On the lecture’s raw additive scale, define

\[ \epsilon=F_{11}-F_{10}-F_{01}+F_{00}. \tag{7.4} \]

The double-mutant change \(F_{11}-F_{00}\) equals the sum of the two single-mutant changes precisely when \(\epsilon=0\). Positive \(\epsilon\) means the combined score is greater than that additive prediction; negative \(\epsilon\) means less. Negative epistasis does not require the double mutant to be worse than the ancestor, or even worse than either single mutant.

The null model must specify its scale. For positive fitness, multiplicative independence uses \(F_{11}/F_{00}=(F_{10}/F_{00})(F_{01}/F_{00})\). Its interaction is additive in log fitness:

\[ \epsilon_{\log}=\ln\frac{F_{11}}{F_{00}}-\ln\frac{F_{10}}{F_{00}} -\ln\frac{F_{01}}{F_{00}} =\ln\frac{F_{11}F_{00}}{F_{10}F_{01}}. \tag{7.5} \]

For values \(1,1.1,1.2,1.32\), raw \(\epsilon=0.02\) but \(\epsilon_{\log}=0\). Calling this landscape epistatic without naming the scale conceals the comparison being made. Ferretti and colleagues explicitly use the log-scale convention when describing their landscape measures; their discussion also distinguishes different measures of ruggedness and evolutionary constraint. Ferretti et al., 2015, introduction pp. 2–4.

Sign epistasis is stronger than a departure from additivity: the same directed mutation is beneficial on one background and deleterious on another. Mutation at the first locus has effects \(F_{10}-F_{00}\) and \(F_{11}-F_{01}\). Their product being negative establishes a sign flip. Reciprocal sign epistasis means both focal mutations flip sign across the other locus’s backgrounds.

Take \(F_{00}=1.0,F_{10}=1.2,F_{01}=1.3,F_{11}=1.1\). The first mutation changes fitness by \(+0.2\) on background 0 at the second locus but by \(-0.2\) on background 1. The second changes it by \(+0.3\) or \(-0.1\). Both flip, so 10 and 01 are distinct local peaks, even though 11 remains better than the ancestor 00. Sign epistasis does not require the double mutant’s absolute value to fall below the ancestor.

Figure 7.1 displays that restricted graph. Starting at 00, a strictly uphill walk can terminate at either peak. A uniform choice between its two improving edges would give probability one-half to each; another mutation or establishment rule could give different probabilities. The height values alone do not determine them.

Epistasis need not produce several peaks. The multiplicative example above has a nonzero raw interaction, yet every 0-to-1 change improves fitness and 11 is its only peak. Ruggedness, often summarised by peak counts, and effect interaction are related but distinct objects. Strictly increasing transformations preserve every edge’s direction and the local peaks, while changing additive interaction values. Neither the presence of epistasis nor a mountain picture alone establishes how predictable a population will be.

Lecture 24 invokes “replaying” evolution as a question about chance and necessity. Our finite models separate endpoint predictability from route predictability: an additive landscape with unique per-locus optima fixes the maximal-walk endpoint without fixing its path; the reciprocal example permits two endpoints under one stated walk rule. These are conditional model statements. They do not turn a thought experiment about life’s history into an observed repeated experiment.

8. Worked exercises

Exercise 1. What a beneficial-only sample cannot tell you

You measure 100 beneficial mutations, 30 of which have \(0.01\le s<0.02\). Estimate the relevant bin probability. Can you infer the probability that a newly generated mutation lies in that bin? What additional quantity is needed?

Solution

The conditional estimate is \(P(0.01\le s<0.02\mid s>0)=0.30\) under the sampling scheme. The unconditional probability is \(0.30P(s>0)\), so the beneficial fraction among the generated mutations is needed, together with compatibility of their sampling weights. Deliberately selecting 100 favourable variants supplies no denominator for all mutations. If detection favours large effects, even the conditional histogram must be corrected or explicitly labelled as a detected-variant distribution.

Exercise 2. Count before drawing

For four variable DNA positions, compute the number of vertices, the degree, the edge count, and the shell at distance two. Compare a four-locus binary restricted landscape.

Solution

The full DNA graph has \(4^4=256\) vertices, degree 12, and \(256(12)/2=1536\) edges. Its distance-two shell contains \(\binom42 3^2=54\) vertices. The binary restriction has \(2^4=16\) vertices, degree four, 32 edges, and a distance-two shell of \(\binom42=6\). A peak inferred from the binary graph has been tested against only four selected neighbours, not all twelve DNA substitutions at those positions or any mutation elsewhere.

Exercise 3. Mutation supply is not fate

There are \(10^{10}\) independent division opportunities with a particular single-change probability \(10^{-9}\). Estimate the expected count and the probability of at least one occurrence. Compare two specified simultaneous changes under independence. Explain why neither calculation establishes fixation.

Solution

The expected single-change count is ten; the Poisson probability is \(1-e^{-10}=0.9999546\). A specified simultaneous double has probability \(10^{-18}\) per opportunity, expected count \(10^{-8}\), and occurrence probability approximately \(10^{-8}\). The second result is tiny but nonzero. Production precedes survival and competition: a generated lineage can be lost. Moreover, the double mutant can arise through sequential changes in a persistent intermediate lineage, so this calculation does not count all routes to it.

Exercise 4. Prove the additive endpoint, then break uniqueness

Prove that every maximal uphill path in the original three-position table ends at GCA. Now change the position-two contribution of A from0.4 to0.6. What part of the conclusion changes?

Solution

Initially each locus has a unique best letter. Any genotype other than GCA has a suboptimal position that can improve through one substitution. Strict increase prevents revisits, so a maximal finite path ends only at GCA. After the change, A and C tie at position two. Both GAA and GCA maximise the total, with the same score1.5, and neither has a fitter neighbour. They are plateau endpoints for a strict-improvement rule, not strict local peaks because they are neutral neighbours. Allowing neutral moves can connect them. Additivity survives but unique sequence prediction does not.

Exercise 5. An interaction that disappears under a scale change

For \(F_{00}=1,F_{10}=1.1,F_{01}=1.2,F_{11}=1.32\), calculate raw and log epistasis and identify all strict peaks. Explain which results survive a strictly increasing transformation.

Solution

Raw interaction is \(1.32-1.1-1.2+1=0.02\). Log interaction is \(\ln[1.32/(1.1\times1.2)]=0\). Both forward substitutions improve on both backgrounds, so11 is the unique strict peak. A strictly increasing transformation preserves all pairwise inequalities, hence beneficial edge directions, sign flips, and peaks. It need not preserve the numerical additive residual or its sign. “No interaction” must therefore be accompanied by the scale and null model used.

Exercise 6. Two peaks, one starting genotype

For the reciprocal landscape in Figure7.1, compute the two background-dependent effects of each mutation. Under a uniform improving-neighbour walk from00, find the terminal distribution. Could one predict that distribution from the four fitness values alone?

Solution

The first-locus effects are \(1.2-1=0.2\) and \(1.1-1.3=-0.2\); the second-locus effects are \(1.3-1=0.3\) and \(1.1-1.2=-0.1\). Both mutations flip sign. From00 the only improving choices are10 and01; each is a strict peak, so the specified uniform rule gives probability0.5 at each endpoint. Fitness labels determine the available uphill edges but do not specify mutation rates, establishment probabilities, or the walk’s transition rule. Different weights can produce a different terminal distribution without changing the landscape. Both double-mutant comparisons reverse despite \(F_{11}>F_{00}\).
