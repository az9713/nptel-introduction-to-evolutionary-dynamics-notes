Sequence comparisons and the design of evolution experiments

Chapter 14 · Lectures 45–46 · From retrospective differences to controlled histories

Two populations carry different DNA sequences. Did selection create those differences, or did neutral mutations drift to fixation? Sequencing identifies the differences, but the difference itself does not identify its cause. Conversely, two cells sampled from a strongly selected population may have the same sequence precisely because a selective sweep removed competing variants. The interpretation depends on what was sampled, over which interval, and under which population model.

Our running construction compares four internal codons in a reference and a second sequence. It is deliberately small enough to enumerate every one-base opportunity. We then design a sequence of growth and transfer cycles, where the ancestry and environment can be recorded as the population changes. The short sequence and all numerical transfer examples are original teaching constructions. They are not a fitted gene, an actual field comparison, or a report of an evolution experiment.

Both complete preferred transcripts, lectures 45 and 46, were read sequentially. They are local machine transcriptions; the listed uploader caption track was not successfully retrieved for these recordings. The chapter retains the lecture's sequence-comparison and experimental-design questions while correcting the neutrality shortcuts, the interpretation of a ratio near one, and the base of the generation-count logarithm. Primary method and population-genetic inspections are identified below.

A difference can be a substitution or a segregating variant L45 · 0:45

Suppose a haploid population contains $N$ individuals, with no selection among the variants being considered. A newly introduced neutral copy has fixation probability $1/N$ in the symmetric models derived earlier. The small probability for one copy does not imply that neutral substitution is negligible in a large population. If a particular site generates neutral mutant origins at rate $N\mu$ per generation, where $\mu$ is its per-copy mutation probability, the expected rate of origins which eventually fix is

\[ (N\mu)\frac1N=\mu. \]

This cancellation separates mutation supply from fixation probability. It is a rate calculation under a neutral, exchangeable model with an appropriate generation clock; it is not a claim that a mutation fixes immediately after appearing. In a rare-mutation setting where origins can be treated separately, it explains why a neutral substitution clock need not depend on census population size. Changing mutation rates, demography, linkage or the neutrality assumption changes the inference.

If two isolated lineages accumulate neutral substitutions at the same rate for $t$ generations after a common ancestor, an infinite-sites idealization gives expected pairwise divergence $2\mu t$ per site. The two branch contributions add. At a finite set of sites, recurrent mutation, reversals and repeated changes can hide earlier events. The same nucleotide change can occur independently in both lineages; it is not impossible merely because the populations are separated. A difference observed today is a net endpoint difference, not necessarily one historical mutation.

A substitution, in this population context, is a variant becoming fixed along a lineage. A polymorphism is variation still segregating within a population. Sequencing two representatives of long-separated lineages and sequencing two contemporaneous cells from one culture therefore measure different objects. The latter comparison may mainly reflect variants that are transient and will eventually disappear. Neither comparison automatically identifies which allele is ancestral; a phylogeny, an outgroup or recorded ancestry can provide that orientation.

Classify changes using the applicable genetic code L45 · 8:25

In a protein-coding sequence, a codon is a three-nucleotide unit read in a specified frame. A synonymous difference changes the codon while retaining the encoded amino acid. A nonsynonymous sense-to-sense difference changes that amino acid. Under the standard code, CCC and CCG both encode proline, while CCC and CAC encode proline and histidine respectively. DNA notation uses T; the corresponding RNA uses U. The comparison must use homologous codons in the correct reading frame.

The NCBI standard genetic-code table, table 1, supplies the assignments used here. The table distinguishes coding assignments from initiation signals, and the same page lists alternative codes. These calculations use internal codons and the standard assignments; they do not classify a change at a start signal merely by its internal amino-acid label. A frame-shifting insertion or deletion is outside this one-base, fixed-frame construction.

Third-position changes are often synonymous, but not always. First-position changes can be synonymous, as in CTG to TTG, both leucine. For the standard code, a single second-position change between sense codons changes the amino acid. Stop-to-stop changes are a separate category, and an alternative code can change the classification. Methionine's ATG and tryptophan's TGG each have one standard sense-codon assignment, so neither has a synonymous one-base neighbor.

Sequence classification and fitness classification must remain distinct. A synonymous change can affect expression, RNA behavior or another biological process without changing the amino-acid sequence. A nonsynonymous change can be effectively neutral under the population size, background and environment being studied. The recording's association of synonymous with neutral and nonsynonymous with nonneutral is a simplifying baseline for a test, not a biological identity. Chapter 20 returns to an experimental test of synonymous effects.

Normalize by opportunities before interpreting counts L45 · 27:09

Each codon has nine distinct one-base neighbors: three positions times three replacements at each position. The opportunities for synonymous changes depend on which codon is present. CCC has three synonymous neighbors, all at its third position, and six amino-acid-changing neighbors. ATG has zero synonymous neighbors. Comparing raw counts from sequences with different codon composition therefore compares different opportunity sets.

Let $K_S$ and $K_N$ be the numbers of synonymous and nonsynonymous sense-to-sense one-base opportunities across the chosen reference segment. Let $D_S$ and $D_N$ be the corresponding observed endpoint differences, with no more than one difference in any compared codon. In an original equal-opportunity, single-endpoint counting construction, define

\[ \widehat\omega_{\rm count} =\frac{D_N/K_N}{D_S/K_S}, \qquad K_S>0,\quad K_N>0,\quad D_S>0. \]

The numerator and denominator are counts divided by their respective opportunity counts. Multiplying both opportunity denominators by the same constant leaves the ratio unchanged. This is the lecture's normalization principle made explicit. It is a teaching count ratio, rather than a complete estimator for an unknown evolutionary history. It uses one specified reference endpoint and treats each one-base opportunity equally.

For our reference segment CCC–CCC–CCC–CCC, $K_S=12$ and $K_N=24$. Compare CCG–CAC–ACC–CCT. The first and fourth changes are synonymous; the second and third change the amino acid. Hence $D_S=D_N=2$, but equal observed counts do not yield a ratio of one:

\[ \widehat\omega_{\rm count} =\frac{2/24}{2/12}=\frac12. \]

There are twice as many amino-acid-changing opportunities in the reference as synonymous ones. Equal counts are therefore lower relative incidence in the former class under this construction. Four codons are far too little information for a confident biological selection inference; the point is the normalization, not a significance claim.

Stop-producing changes require an explicit convention. Our program counts them separately and excludes them from the sense-to-sense $K_N$. For TGG, the opportunities are zero synonymous, seven amino-acid replacements and two stops, totaling nine. The CCC running segment has no one-base stop neighbor, so this convention does not alter its result. A sequence analysis must specify how stops, ambiguous bases, gaps and multiple differences are treated, rather than silently assigning them ordinary amino-acid replacements.

Nei and Gojobori (1986), printed pages 418–420, develop a fuller counting method. They count synonymous and nonsynonymous sites using fractions of possible changes, average the opportunities of the two compared sequences, and handle codons differing at multiple positions by possible paths. Their example TTT to GTA has two shortest paths with different synonymous counts; equal path weighting yields one-half synonymous and one-and-one-half nonsynonymous differences. They then apply an approximate multiple-hit correction. The manuscript's single-reference ratio omits those steps and must not be represented as their complete method.

Mutation biases also matter. If transitions, transversions or particular contexts have different rates, unweighted neighbor counts are an incomplete neutral expectation. More developed codon models represent substitution histories and estimate rates under explicit assumptions. In such models, $d_N$ and $d_S$ describe inferred nonsynonymous and synonymous substitutions per corresponding site, and $\omega=d_N/d_S$ is a rate ratio. Observed endpoint counts need not equal historical substitution counts when a site changes several times.

A ratio tests a model; it does not partition evolution into two forces L46 · 3:21

For suitably diverged coding lineages, with synonymous sites providing an appropriate neutral baseline and an adequate substitution model, $\omega>1$ can support selection promoting amino-acid substitutions. A ratio below one can support purifying selection, which removes many amino-acid-changing variants. Both inferences concern relative substitution rates. Drift still acts on individual selected variants, and selection can be intense when the ratio is below one. The ratio is not the percentage of change caused by selection divided by the percentage caused by drift.

A ratio near one is compatible with a neutral baseline in that model, but it does not establish that every site is neutral or that the gene is functionally unused. Different sites or historical intervals can be under different pressures. A whole-gene average can conceal selected sites, while statistical uncertainty, inadequate mutation modeling and selected synonymous sites can alter the comparison. A transporter unnecessary for one resource might still incur expression costs, serve another function or become relevant when conditions change. The lecture's galactose-versus-glucose example is a conditional thought experiment, not a universal neutrality result.

The sampling distinction is particularly consequential. Kryazhimskiy and Plotkin (2008), PDF pages 1–4, derive different relationships for fixed differences between divergent lineages and for variants sampled within one population. Their actual Figure 1 compares those relationships under their stated model. For within-population samples, the expected ratio need not vary monotonically with selection strength. Strong positive selection can reduce contemporaneous nonsynonymous variation through rapid sweeps; a low within-population ratio can therefore have a different explanation from a low substitution ratio between lineages.

This result repairs the inference that two closely related genomes with a ratio near one demonstrate drift alone. Before interpreting the number, identify whether the differences are fixed between lineages or segregating within a shared population. Then specify the mutation process, the neutral reference, the sampling interval and the model. The paper's theoretical curves are not a calibration for our four-codon construction, and no selection coefficient is inferred from that tiny example.

Zero synonymous differences are another boundary. If $D_S=0$, the simple count ratio is undefined; writing “infinity” and calling it proof of positive selection mistakes a missing denominator for evidence. Likewise, a coding segment with no synonymous opportunities supplies no synonymous baseline. Larger samples, an explicit likelihood model and uncertainty estimates may be needed. Adding an arbitrary pseudocount changes the estimator and must be disclosed.

Controlled histories answer questions that endpoint comparisons leave open L46 · 12:18

The course now moves from reconstructing an unknown history to observing evolution under a recorded environment. An experimental lineage has a known starting population, a specified resource and transfer regime, and samples collected as it changes. Microbes can process many reproductive generations in a comparatively short calendar interval, and multiple populations can be followed under nominally identical treatments. Short generation time alone does not make different populations follow the same path: mutation arrivals, random transfer and competition remain stochastic.

A useful experiment distinguishes the unit receiving a treatment from the unit measured. Independent evolving populations are biological replicates for a question about repeatability. Several sequencing libraries from one population mainly replicate measurement or preparation; they do not create several independent evolutionary histories. Shared stocks and environments can introduce dependencies, so the origin of each replicate should be recorded. A persistent contamination or systematic assay bias is not repaired merely by collecting many readings of it.

Periodic frozen samples allow earlier and later populations to be revived and compared under a common assay environment. A whole-population archive can retain multiple genotypes; a single isolated clone records only one genotype. Recovery from an archive can itself introduce sampling and growth effects. The archive makes a comparison possible, but does not prove that every rare lineage was preserved or that revival is biologically invisible. Record sampling times, archive type, recovery conditions and which population or clone entered each assay.

Comparing an evolved population with its ancestor in the same assay helps separate inherited changes from immediate environmental effects. Even then, performance is conditional on the assay. Faster use of one carbon source, improved survival in stationary phase and altered competitive interactions are distinct outcomes. A genome difference is a candidate explanation; recreating or removing the candidate in a specified genetic background is a stronger causal test than correlation alone. The later experimental chapters examine concrete applications and their limitations.

The lectures introduce three designs. Serial transfer repeatedly exposes populations to a growth cycle followed by a bottleneck. A chemostat supplies and removes material continuously, with its physical balances derived in chapter 6. Mutation-accumulation designs use severe bottlenecks to reduce the efficacy of selection on many mutations, rather than guaranteeing that all selection disappears. These regimes control different processes; they are not interchangeable ways of holding every population parameter constant.

Count reproductive doublings through growth and transfer L46 · 22:55

In the lecture's serial-transfer sketch, a population begins a growth interval with $N_{\rm in}$ cells and ends with $N_{\rm out}$. Under ideal binary fission with no deaths or immigration, the number of net population doublings $G$ satisfies $N_{\rm out}=N_{\rm in}2^G$. Taking a logarithm gives

\[ G=\log_2\frac{N_{\rm out}}{N_{\rm in}} =\frac{\ln(N_{\rm out}/N_{\rm in})}{\ln2}. \]

The ASR contains both a fold-ratio and a natural-log shorthand; neither is the complete doubling formula. A thousandfold increase is approximately 9.966 doublings, not 1,000 generations and not $\ln1000$ generations. If birth and death both occur, net increase undercounts the total divisions and does not by itself recover every cell's ancestral generation count. During a stationary interval, a flat count does not prove that no births, deaths or selection occur.

Let $b=N_{\rm in}/N_{\rm out}$ be the realized inoculum fraction in a cycle which returns to the same endpoint count. Then $G=\log_2(1/b)$. With $b=1/1000$, thirty such cycles yield about 298.974 net doublings. With $b=1/100$, they yield about 199.316. These are illustrative mathematical regimes. Changing the endpoint yield, inoculum count or resource conditions changes the clock; calendar days should not be converted into generations without checking those quantities.

A fixed transferred volume does not specify an exact number of viable cells unless density, mixing and viability are known. The lecture's volume fraction motivates an expected sampling fraction. To expose the bottleneck consequence, consider instead an explicit original model: take $B=1000$ independent cell draws with replacement from a large, well-mixed source. A rare genotype at frequency $x=0.001$ has expected transferred count $Bx=1$, yet its probability of being absent is

\[ \Pr(\hbox{no rare carrier})=(1-x)^B =(0.999)^{1000}\approx0.367695. \]

An expectation of one carrier does not guarantee one carrier. Real sampling without replacement uses a hypergeometric law when the finite source counts are specified; random transferred cell counts introduce another distribution. The model is chosen to illustrate stochastic loss, not to claim that pipetting physically replaces sampled cells. A lineage that grows successfully inside a cycle can still fail to enter the next cycle.

This connects experimental design to the earlier clocks. Growth supplies mutations and changes relative abundance; transfer changes which lineages continue. The relevant effective population size can depend on the fluctuating cycle and reproduction rule, rather than being equal to either the final count or the bottleneck count by definition. Archive placement within a cycle and assay placement after recovery should therefore be part of the scientific record.

Reproduce the opportunity and passage calculations

The standard-library companion chapter14_sequence_and_passage.py constructs the 64 standard codon assignments, enumerates all nine neighbors, separates stop-producing opportunities, and rejects ambiguous or stop inputs. It also rejects multiple endpoint differences in one codon, which need an evolutionary-path model. The count ratio requires nonzero opportunities in both classes and at least one observed synonymous difference. Its explicit limits prevent the toy calculation from masquerading as a complete sequence-analysis package.

This runnable listing uses that local companion. Inputs are uppercase DNA codon tuples, a finite fold increase at least one, and a nonnegative integer number of cycles. It produces exact rational counting output and floating-point doubling/sampling values. It reads no biological dataset, creates no files, and does not estimate a statistical confidence interval.

from chapter14_sequence_and_passage import (
    count_ratio, opportunities, passage_clock,
)

reference = ("CCC",) * 4
comparison = ("CCG", "CAC", "ACC", "CCT")
ks, kn, ds, dn, ratio = count_ratio(reference, comparison)
print(f"K_S={ks}; K_N={kn}; D_S={ds}; D_N={dn}")
print(f"normalized_count_ratio={ratio}")
print(f"CCC opportunities={opportunities('CCC')}")
print(f"TGG opportunities={opportunities('TGG')}")
for fold in (100, 1000):
    print(f"fold={fold}; 30-cycle doublings="
          f"{passage_clock(fold, 30):.6f}")
print(f"miss_at_bottleneck={(1 - 0.001) ** 1000:.6f}")

Six exercises with full solutions

1. Audit a codon opportunity count

Under the standard code, classify CCC to CCA and CCC to ACC. Count all synonymous and sense-to-sense nonsynonymous one-base neighbors of CCC. Why can this result not be applied to every codon?

Solution

CCC and CCA encode proline, so the first change is synonymous. ACC encodes threonine, so the second changes the amino acid. The three third-position replacements, CCT, CCA and CCG, all retain proline. The six replacements at the first two positions encode other amino acids and none is a stop, yielding $(K_S,K_N,K_{\rm stop})=(3,6,0)$ for this codon. Other codons have different assignments: TGG has no synonymous neighbor and two one-base stop neighbors. Opportunity normalization must use the actual sequence and an explicit stop convention.

2. Repair a raw-count interpretation

A hypothetical comparison has $K_S=100$, $K_N=400$, $D_S=4$ and $D_N=8$. Calculate the teaching count ratio. An analyst instead uses $D_N/D_S=2$ to claim positive selection. Diagnose the inference, and explain what happens if $D_S=0$.

Solution

The normalized ratio is $(8/400)/(4/100)=0.5$, while the raw ratio is two. Four times as many nonsynonymous opportunities make raw counts an inappropriate baseline comparison. The normalized value is consistent with lower nonsynonymous incidence relative to this equal-opportunity baseline; it does not by itself provide a statistical selection test or validate the mutation model. If $D_S=0$, the denominator is zero and this count ratio is undefined. A pseudocount or a likelihood estimate would be an additional method, whose assumptions and uncertainty must be specified.

3. Explain why one does not prove absence of selection

Two equally weighted site classes have the same synonymous rate but nonsynonymous-to-synonymous rate ratios 0.2 and 1.8. Find their pooled rate ratio. Give a second reason why a ratio near one from two contemporaneous cells need not establish neutral evolution.

Solution

Let each class have synonymous rate $a>0$ and equal numbers of sites. Total nonsynonymous rate is $(0.2+1.8)a=2a$, while total synonymous rate is $2a$, so the pooled ratio is one. A mixture can therefore conceal differing site-specific pressures even when the pooled normalization is appropriate. For two contemporaneous cells, the differences can be segregating polymorphisms rather than fixed lineage substitutions. Their population-genetic relationship to selection differs; the Kryazhimskiy–Plotkin model even permits a near-one ratio under nonneutral selection. Neither argument proves selection in a given dataset; each defeats the inference that one alone proves its absence.

4. Separate supply from the neutral clock

Two separate neutral haploid populations have sizes $10^5$ and $10^7$. Both have per-site mutation probability $\mu=10^{-8}$ per generation. Under the rare-origin, exchangeable neutral construction, compare successful-origin substitution rates. If both branches persist for 1,000 generations after a common ancestor, what expected per-site divergence follows under an infinite-sites approximation?

Solution

The first supplies $10^{-3}$ origins per generation with fixation probability $10^{-5}$ each, giving $10^{-8}$ successful origins per generation. The second supplies $10^{-1}$ origins with probability $10^{-7}$ each, giving the same rate. With identical branch clocks, expected divergence is $2(10^{-8})(1000)=2\times10^{-5}$ per site. This is an expectation, not a guaranteed difference at one site. Rare-origin separability, no selected effects, no migration and no repeated-site masking are among the idealizations; origin success and the later time at which fixation finishes are distinct.

5. Correct the serial-transfer clock

Each cycle starts with $10^4$ viable cells and ends with $10^7$. Assuming binary growth without death, calculate net doublings per cycle and over 30 cycles. What additional difficulty arises if the endpoint count stays flat while births and deaths continue?

Solution

The fold increase is $10^3$. Net doublings are $\log_2(1000)\approx9.965784$ per cycle and $30\log_2(1000)\approx298.973529$ across the thirty identical cycles. The expression $\ln1000$ must be divided by $\ln2$. If births and deaths continue at a flat count, the net ratio is one and its logarithm is zero even though actual divisions occurred. Endpoint counts then cannot determine cumulative divisions or all lineage generation counts. A demographic measurement or a model for turnover is needed.

6. Decide what an archive and a replicate preserve

A rare genotype has frequency 0.001 before an archive is made by 1,000 independent with-replacement cell draws. Find its absence probability. Explain why sequencing ten aliquots of that one archive does not provide ten independent evolution replicates, and why a single-clone archive answers a different question.

Solution

The absence probability is $(0.999)^{1000}\approx0.367695$, despite expected rare count one. Ten aliquots share the same preceding evolutionary and archive-sampling history; they can characterize measurement variability or the retained mixture, but they do not replicate independent mutation, growth and transfer histories. A single clone records one sampled genotype and cannot preserve population diversity by itself. Recovering a genotype from an archive demonstrates that it was retained and recovered, while failing to recover it may reflect sampling or recovery rather than absence from the original population.

Carry the model assumptions into the experiment

Sequence comparisons show accumulated or segregating change under an observation model. Serial transfer supplies a recorded sequence of growth, bottlenecks and samples. Neither design makes selection, drift and mutation independently visible without further reasoning. The next chapters use concrete culture platforms and primary experiments to determine which measurements distinguish competing explanations.
