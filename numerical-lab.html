Numerical laboratory: survival, fixation, and growth

A mutant can reproduce faster than its competitors and still disappear. To assess an introduction of one mutant into fifty individuals, we need two answers: its chance of eventually occupying the whole population, and the time until either extinction or fixation. Neither answer follows from a deterministic growth curve. This laboratory separates the finite-population question from the growth-rate question, then makes the assumptions and units visible in executable models.

Provenance. All examples, controls, plots, and code on this page are original teaching constructions. They illustrate mathematical models associated with the course's growth, selection, and finite-population topics. They are not measurements of E. coli, fitted biological parameters, a reproduction of an experiment, or evidence that a recording stated these numerical values. The Python implementation uses only the standard library; the browser calculations run locally.

1. What one replacement event means

Let $N$ be the fixed number of haploid individuals: each carries one set of its genome. Each individual inherits either the resident type A or the mutant type B. Let $I_k$ be the number of mutants after $k$ attempted birth-and-replacement events. Its state space is the integers $0,1,\ldots,N$. We assume no mutation, no migration, no spatial structure, and no environmental change. A mutant has constant relative reproductive fitness $r\gt0$ and a resident has fitness $1$. Fitness here is a dimensionless weight for choosing a parent; it is not a growth rate in inverse hours.

At each event we first sample a parent with probability proportional to its fitness. Independently, we sample one replacement target uniformly from all $N$ individuals. The offspring inherits the parent's type and replaces the target. Sampling independently permits the parent to be its own replacement target. It also permits one mutant to replace another mutant or one resident to replace another resident. These events leave the mutant count unchanged, but they consume an event of time. A self-loop below means any event that leaves the count unchanged; it is broader than an individual replacing itself.

The total parental fitness at state $i$ is $ri+N-i$. Define $p_i^+$ as the probability that the count increases by one, $p_i^-$ as the probability that it decreases by one, and $p_i^0$ as the probability that it stays at $i$. An increase requires a mutant parent and a resident replacement target. Independence therefore gives

$$p_i^+=\frac{ri}{ri+N-i}\frac{N-i}{N},\qquad p_i^-=\frac{N-i}{ri+N-i}\frac{i}{N},\qquad p_i^0=1-p_i^+-p_i^-.$$

At $i=0$ and $i=N$, both probabilities of changing the count are zero. The process remains at that boundary forever: these are absorbing states. For every interior state, both change probabilities are positive. Their ratio is $p_i^-/p_i^+=1/r$. Selection changes the relative likelihood of the two directions; random sampling remains even when $r\gt1$. This birth-first, fitness-weighted update is the convention described by the primary research article on environmental fitness heterogeneity, here specialized to a homogeneous environment.

The time convention matters before any calculation. In this lab, one unit of time is one attempted event, including events that leave the count unchanged. A nominal generation is defined as $N$ attempted events. This division is a reporting convention, not an experimentally calibrated mapping to hours and not a claim that each individual reproduces exactly once.

2. Derive the chance of fixation

Fixation means reaching the all-mutant state $N$ before the all-resident state $0$. Let $u_i$ be this probability conditional on starting at $I_0=i$. The boundary conditions are $u_0=0$ and $u_N=1$. Conditioning on the first event gives $u_i=p_i^+u_{i+1}+p_i^-u_{i-1}+p_i^0u_i$. Subtracting $u_i$ and grouping differences isolates the step where self-loops cancel:

$$p_i^+(u_{i+1}-u_i)=p_i^-(u_i-u_{i-1}).$$

Define the increment $d_i=u_i-u_{i-1}$ for $1\le i\le N$. Dividing by $p_i^+$ in an interior state gives $d_{i+1}=r^{-1}d_i$, so $d_j=d_1r^{-(j-1)}$. Summing from state zero to state $i$ produces $u_i=d_1\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}r^{-j}$. The condition at state $N$ determines $d_1$ by normalizing the same sum over $N$ terms. Consequently,

$$u_i=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}r^{-j}}{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}r^{-j}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1-r^{-i}}{1-r^{-N}},&r\ne1,\\\displaystyle\frac{i}{N},&r=1.\end{cases}$$

The neutral result is not an approximation: equal fitness makes all increments equal, and the two boundaries force $u_i=i/N$. With a single neutral mutant in fifty individuals, the probability of fixation is $0.02$. Giving that mutant fitness $1.1$ raises the probability to approximately $0.09169$. The mutant still goes extinct in approximately $90.83\%$ of introductions under these assumptions. A reproductive advantage changes odds; it does not make initial rarity disappear.

The fixation calculation needs only the ratio of the two transition probabilities. It cannot determine how often a change occurs. Indeed, multiplying both change probabilities by the same state-dependent factor and adjusting the stay probability leaves the equation for $u_i$ unchanged while changing the clock. Kluth and Baake, Section 2, equation (1), give a continuous-time Moran model with upward and downward rates in the ratio $r:1$ for the fitter type. Conditional on one attempted reproduction event, dividing both rates by the total reproduction rate $ri+N-i$ recovers our transition probabilities. The two clocks have different units, but their fixation probabilities agree. Our backward-equation derivation establishes the cancellation directly for the full domain $r\gt0$.

Directly evaluating $1-r^{-i}$ near $r=1$ subtracts nearly equal floating-point numbers. The implementation evaluates the same formula using log(r) and expm1, where expm1(z) computes $e^z-1$ accurately when $z$ is small. For $r\lt1$, it rescales the expression so that no large positive exponential is required. At $r=1$ it evaluates $i/N$ exactly in the model. These rearrangements improve numerical evaluation; they do not change the mathematical process.

3. Keep the clock while solving absorption time

Let $\tau=\min\{k\ge0:I_k\in\{0,N\}\}$ be the first attempted-event index at which either boundary is reached. Let $T_i=\mathbb{E}[\tau\mid I_0=i]$ denote the unconditional mean absorption time. It includes trajectories ending at extinction and trajectories ending at fixation. It is not the mean fixation time among successful mutants. The latter conditions on a different set of trajectories and needs a different calculation.

At either boundary $T_0=T_N=0$. In an interior state, the first event costs one unit even when nothing changes. Conditioning on its outcome therefore gives $T_i=1+p_i^+T_{i+1}+p_i^-T_{i-1}+p_i^0T_i$. Rearrangement yields the linear system

$$-p_i^-T_{i-1}+(p_i^++p_i^-)T_i-p_i^+T_{i+1}=1,\qquad 1\le i\le N-1.$$

This system is tridiagonal: a row couples only adjacent mutant counts. The code eliminates the lower diagonal from left to right, then substitutes from right to left. It stores and performs a constant amount of work per state, so both time and memory grow proportionally to $N$. Including $p_i^++p_i^-$ on the diagonal preserves the waiting time caused by self-loops. Replacing it by $1$ would solve a different clock and would be wrong for attempted-event time.

A two-individual neutral population gives an independent check without a numerical solver. In its only interior state, $p_1^+=p_1^-=1/4$ and $p_1^0=1/2$. An event reaches either boundary with probability $1/2$, so the number of attempted events until absorption has mean $2$. If we counted only actual changes, the mean would be $1$. This small case detects the most consequential convention error.

For another exact check, define $H_m=\sum_{j=1}^{m}1/j$, with $H_0=0$. Neutrality gives $p_i^+=p_i^-=i(N-i)/N^2$. At $i=1$, the mean absorption time is $T_1=NH_{N-1}$. To see why, write $e_i=T_i-T_{i-1}$. The recurrence gives $e_{i+1}-e_i=-N^2/[i(N-i)]$. Summing these differences and using $\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_i=T_N-T_0=0$ determines $e_1=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(N-j)N/[j(N-j)]=NH_{N-1}$. Since $T_0=0$, $e_1=T_1$. In fifty individuals this is approximately $223.9603$ attempted events, or $4.4792$ nominal generations.

Near a boundary the next event usually preserves the count. In our fifty-individual neutral example at $i=1$, $p_1^0=0.9608$, so the mean wait until the next count change is $1/(1-p_1^0)\approx25.5102$ attempted events. This local wait is not the full absorption time: after the first change, subsequent interior states may require further waiting.

4. Growth adds physical units and changes population size

The fixed-$N$ model cannot describe an expanding culture. For a separate growth model, let $X(t)$ be a deterministic cell count at physical time $t$ in hours, let $X_0=X(0)$, and let $g\ge0$ be a per-capita growth rate in inverse hours. Assuming every cell contributes the same constant expected growth gives $dX/dt=gX$. Dividing by $X$ and integrating gives $\log X(t)-\log X_0=gt$, hence $X(t)=X_0e^{gt}$ for positive $X_0$. The zero initial condition remains zero. The product $gt$ must be dimensionless; inserting hours into a rate expressed per minute changes the result by a factor of sixty in the exponent.

To introduce a resource ceiling, let $K\gt0$ be a carrying capacity in cells and replace the growth law by $dX/dt=gX(1-X/K)$. For $0\lt X_0\lt K$, separation of variables gives $\log[X/(K-X)]-\log[X_0/(K-X_0)]=gt$. Solving for $X$ yields

$$X(t)=\frac{K}{1+\left((K-X_0)/X_0\right)e^{-gt}}.$$

The carrying-capacity boundary stays fixed at $X=K$ and the zero boundary stays at zero. At low abundance the factor $1-X/K$ is close to one, so logistic growth initially resembles exponential growth. With $X_0=100$ cells, $g=0.3$ per hour, $K=10{,}000$ cells, and $t=10$ hours, exponential growth gives approximately $2008.55$ cells, while logistic growth gives approximately $1686.65$ cells. These noninteger values are deterministic model expectations or continuous approximations, not an enumeration of individual cells.

Density limitation also warns against treating $r$ and $g$ as interchangeable. Moran fitness chooses a parent while holding the population size fixed. An exponential growth rate governs change in population abundance per unit physical time. A conversion between the two requires an explicitly specified demographic model; no such conversion is built into this laboratory.

5. Deterministic selection acts on log odds

Suppose the resident type A and mutant type B grow independently at constant rates $g_A$ and $g_B$, both in inverse hours. Let $A(t)$ and $B(t)$ be their deterministic abundances and $x(t)=B(t)/[A(t)+B(t)]$ the mutant frequency. Define $\Delta g=g_B-g_A$. The ratio of the two exponential solutions is $B(t)/A(t)=[B(0)/A(0)]e^{\Delta g t}$. Because $B/A=x/(1-x)$, selection makes the mutant frequency's odds grow exponentially:

$$\frac{x(t)}{1-x(t)}=\frac{x_0}{1-x_0}e^{\Delta g t},\qquad x(t)=\frac{1}{1+[(1-x_0)/x_0]e^{-\Delta g t}}.$$

Here $x_0$ is the initial frequency, between zero and one. A zero or unit initial frequency stays at that endpoint. For $x_0=0.02$, $\Delta g=0.1$ per hour, and $t=10$ hours, the final frequency is approximately $0.05256$. That number is a frequency along a deterministic trajectory. It is not a probability of eventual fixation. In particular, the deterministic solution stays strictly inside the two endpoints at any finite time when it starts inside them. Sampling in the finite Moran process can instead hit an endpoint in finitely many events.

6. Reproduce the calculation and inspect its contract

The downloadable Python source implements the models derived above. moran_transition(n, i, r) returns the up, down, and stay probabilities for one attempted event. fixation_probability(n, i, r) returns the probability of reaching the all-mutant state. mean_absorption_times(n, r) returns an array indexed by every possible starting count, including zero at both boundaries. The three deterministic functions return abundance or frequency at a supplied time; the rate and time inputs must use reciprocal units.

The finite-population inputs require an integer population size at least one, an integer initial count between zero and that size, and finite positive relative fitness. Booleans are rejected even though Python treats them as integer subclasses. Invalid types raise TypeError; invalid domains raise ValueError. The logistic model restricts the initial abundance to the interval from zero to capacity and uses a nonnegative rate. The exponential model permits negative rates to represent decline. All functions are pure numerical calculations: they make no network requests, read no files, and modify no data. Running the source as a script prints a JSON worked example.

from numerical_lab import fixation_probability, mean_absorption_times

n, i, r = 50, 1, 1.1
probability = fixation_probability(n, i, r)
events = mean_absorption_times(n, r)[i]
print(f"fixation probability: {probability:.8f}")
print(f"mean absorption events: {events:.6f}")
print(f"nominal generations: {events / n:.6f}")

Numerical validation compares fixation probabilities to independent sums of geometric terms and absorption times to an independently assembled dense linear system. Exact neutral boundaries and the two-individual check test the conventions rather than only the implementation. Such checks establish consistency with the stated equations; they do not validate the equations as a model of a specific organism. Ordinary double-precision arithmetic also cannot resolve every probability arbitrarily close to zero or one; values below representable precision may round to a boundary.

7. Which question each model can answer

ModelState and clockQuestion answeredMissing mechanism
Finite Moran processInteger mutant count; attempted eventsChance and unconditional time to either boundaryPopulation expansion, mutation, spatial structure, environmental feedback
Exponential growthContinuous abundance; hoursAbundance under constant per-capita growthFinite resources and demographic randomness
Logistic growthContinuous abundance; hoursAbundance under a specified density ceilingMechanistic resource dynamics and type competition
Two-type differential growthContinuous frequency; hoursFrequency trajectory under a constant rate differenceRandom loss of rare types and finite-time stochastic fixation

Matching initial frequencies across the models does not make their clocks or endpoints equivalent. To construct a richer model, choose which missing mechanism matters for the biological question, state its update rule, and derive new probabilities or differential equations. Adding positive mutation rates in both directions to the Moran process, for example, destroys both absorbing boundaries. Its eventual stationary distribution then answers a different question from fixation without mutation. With mutation in only one direction, the boundary containing exclusively the type that cannot mutate back remains absorbing.

8. Exercises with worked solutions

1. Derive the drift in one attempted event

Compute $\mathbb{E}[I_{k+1}-I_k\mid I_k=i]$. Identify the conditions under which it is positive. Does positive drift prove eventual fixation?

Worked solution

The count increment is $1$, $-1$, or $0$, so its conditional mean is $p_i^+-p_i^-$. Substituting the two transition probabilities gives $i(N-i)(r-1)/[N(ri+N-i)]$. At an interior state the denominator and $i(N-i)$ are positive, so the sign is the sign of $r-1$. Positive conditional mean does not eliminate downward steps: $p_i^-$ remains positive. The fixation formula gives a probability strictly below one for any interior initial count and finite positive fitness.

2. Neutrality with three individuals

For $N=3$ and $r=1$, compute $u_1,u_2,T_1,T_2$ without executing the solver.

Worked solution

Neutrality gives $u_1=1/3$ and $u_2=2/3$. In each interior state, the up and down probabilities are $2/9$. By reflection symmetry, $T_1=T_2=T$. The first row of the backward system is $(4/9)T_1-(2/9)T_2=1$, hence $(2/9)T=1$ and both mean times are $9/2$ attempted events. Dividing by three gives $1.5$ nominal generations. The harmonic check gives $T_1=3H_2=3(1+1/2)=9/2$.

3. Preserve fixation and change duration

Modify each interior transition by replacing $p_i^+$ and $p_i^-$ with half their original values and increasing $p_i^0$ to keep the probabilities normalized. Find the new fixation probability and mean absorption time.

Worked solution

The ratio of down to up probabilities remains unchanged, so the homogeneous backward equation for fixation has exactly the same boundary-value solution. The time equation instead becomes half the original tridiagonal matrix times the new time vector equal to the vector of ones. Therefore its solution is twice the original time vector. The extra waiting changes duration while preserving the eventual choice of boundary.

4. Diagnose an incompatible rate comparison

A mutant has Moran fitness $r=1.1$. A second model assigns it a growth advantage of $0.1$ per hour. Can the two values be identified without another assumption?

Worked solution

No. The first number is a dimensionless relative parent-selection weight in a fixed-size population. The second is a difference between abundance growth rates with dimensions of inverse time. If one postulates a common generation time and defines relative multiplication over that interval, one can construct an exponential relation such as a ratio $e^{\Delta g t}$, but that is an additional demographic model. It is not implied by either number alone.

5. Infer a deterministic rate difference

A deterministic two-type model starts at frequency $0.02$ and reaches frequency $0.1$ after ten hours. Infer $\Delta g$ and state what would invalidate this inference.

Worked solution

Take logarithms of the odds equation: $\Delta g=[\log(0.1/0.9)-\log(0.02/0.98)]/10=\log(49/9)/10\approx0.16946$ per hour. This value assumes constant independent exponential rates, no mutation, and no sampling drift. Time-varying rates or strong stochastic changes in a small population would invalidate interpreting it as one constant biological rate difference.

6. Design a check that catches the wrong clock

A program produces the correct neutral fixation probability for every initial count but reports a mean absorption time of one event for $N=2,i=1,r=1$. Identify the probable error and propose an independent check.

Worked solution

The program likely counts only changes rather than attempted events. Its fixation result can remain correct because self-loops cancel from the equation for fixation. Assemble the one-state transition matrix directly: the transient stay probability is $1/2$, and the expected time satisfies $T=1+(1/2)T$, yielding $T=2$. This check uses the actual attempted-event convention and detects the error without trusting the program's recurrence.

Environmental fitness heterogeneity in the Moran process, Section 2, states the fitness-weighted birth and random replacement convention and identifies its homogeneous special case.
