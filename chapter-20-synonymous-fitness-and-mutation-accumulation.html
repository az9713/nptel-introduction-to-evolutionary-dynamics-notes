20. Synonymous fitness effects, mutation accumulation, and the limits of a lineage model

Lecture 60 · Recording · Prerequisites: codon classification in chapter 14, growth and bottlenecks in chapters 5–6, and finite-population sampling in chapters 9–11.

A sequence change can preserve the amino acid sequence and still change what a cell produces. A population can pass through severe bottlenecks and still experience selection during growth. A community can retain several competitors because the advantage of each depends on who else is present. These observations have a common structure: a coarse description suppresses variables that matter to the observable being measured. The final lecture returns to those variables, starting inside the ribosome and ending with communities and transferred DNA.

This chapter follows that order. The biochemical and experimental examples motivate original models whose assumptions can be inspected and calculations reproduced. None of the numerical examples below is an estimate fitted to the lecture or an empirical dataset. The community and gene-transfer models are extensions of the closing questions, rather than models asserted by the recording.

A synonymous change preserves a sequence, not every phenotype

Lecture 60, 00:46–03:45. Synonymous means that the applicable genetic code translates the changed codon into the same amino acid. It does not mean that two messenger RNAs have the same secondary structure, lifetime, accessibility to a ribosome, or pattern of translation elongation. Nor does it imply that the resulting cells have equal competitive fitness. Chapter 14 separated the sequence classification from a population-genetic neutrality assumption; here we give the separation a mechanistic interpretation.

A useful causal chain is DNA sequence, RNA production and persistence, translation, protein folding and degradation, biochemical activity, and finally reproduction in an environment. Preserving one link—the amino acid sequence—does not fix all the others. A tightly structured initiation region can affect access to the translation machinery. Changes in RNA lifetime can alter how many transcripts are available. Changes in the time at which parts of a nascent chain emerge can alter folding. These are possible routes, not a claim that every synonymous change has a measurable effect through every route.

Codon rarity is also conditional. A codon that is uncommon in a genome is not a universal stopwatch with a fixed translation delay. Transfer-RNA abundance and charging, nearby sequence, cellular state, and the experimental expression system can matter. A useful experiment therefore measures relevant intermediates rather than inferring the entire mechanism from a change in fluorescence or growth alone. Equal fluorescence need not establish equal protein amount, and unequal protein amount need not establish unequal organismal fitness.

Classification and inference. “Synonymous” is a statement about translation under a specified code. “Neutral” is a statement about an effect on reproductive success under specified demographic and environmental conditions. The former does not logically imply the latter.

Translation creates a time window for folding

Lecture 60, 03:45–10:13. During translation the amino-terminal part of a chain appears before its carboxy-terminal part. Folding can begin while some residues have not yet emerged from the ribosome. A whole-chain refolding experiment begins from a different ensemble: regions that were unavailable during synthesis may now be available simultaneously. A kinetically trapped outcome can therefore depend on the path through which the same final chain was assembled.

The lecture illustrates this using competing fluorescent structures. The identified primary study, Sander, Chaney and Clark (2014), uses an engineered yellow–black–blue construct, YKB, with three half-domains. The central K half-domain can participate in either an amino-terminal YK structure or a carboxy-terminal KB structure. Its yellow/blue fluorescence should be distinguished from the lecture’s green/red schematic. The primary result concerns competing folded structures and synonymous changes in a particular coding region; it does not demonstrate that synonymous changes generally create wholly new protein structures. Sander et al., main text and Figures 1–3.

The primary article also reports that conventional pulse-chase measurements did not resolve a significant change in the variants’ overall synthesis rate. Thus an account of local timing is not a license to replace the experiment with a measured long global translation arrest. The following calculation is a deliberately simpler timing construction, useful for seeing how a window can matter even when sequence identity is preserved.

Suppose a partially available chain folds irreversibly into an amino-terminal structure with constant hazard $k$ during a window of length $\tau$. The hazard has units of inverse time. Let $S(t)$ be the probability that folding has not occurred by time $t$. If the chance of folding in a short interval $dt$, conditional on remaining unfolded, is $k\,dt+o(dt)$, then

$$S(t+dt)=S(t)(1-k\,dt)+o(dt),\qquad S'(t)=-kS(t),\qquad S(0)=1.$$

Integrating gives $S(t)=e^{-kt}$. The probability that this route has already captured the chain before the competing region becomes available is therefore

$$p_{\mathrm{early}}=1-e^{-k\tau}. \tag{20.1}$$

The dimensionless product $k\tau$ controls the answer. If $k\tau\ll1$, the probability is approximately $k\tau$; if $k\tau\gg1$, it approaches one. With $k=2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, changing $\tau$ from $0.2$ to $1$ second changes the probability from about $0.330$ to $0.865$. These are synthetic values. Equation (20.1) does not represent all of YKB: it omits unfolding, chaperones, alternative folding intermediates and competition after the window. In particular, the probability of early capture is not automatically the final fraction of a fluorescent state unless the subsequent kinetics make it so.

Random transfers reduce selection without abolishing it

Lecture 60, 10:13–18:40. In a mutation-accumulation experiment, independently propagated lines repeatedly pass through a narrow bottleneck. The lecture describes marking a plate before colonies become visible, choosing the colony at or nearest that preselected region, resuspending it, and sampling cells for the next plate. Preselection helps avoid choosing a conspicuously large or unusual colony. Resuspension helps avoid deliberately sampling only a colony’s center or edge. The details matter because an apparently random transfer can still contain phenotypic preferences.

Several different sample sizes occur in that description. The number of cells spread on a plate, the number founding one chosen colony, and the number ultimately contributing to the ancestry of the next selected colony are distinct. If colonies arise from separated single cells, choosing one colony imposes a single-founder ancestry bottleneck even though roughly a hundred cells were initially spread across the plate. If cells aggregate, colonies merge, or sampling favors an accessible subpopulation, the ideal single-founder interpretation may fail. Transferred volume alone does not establish any of these counts.

Mutations must also survive the growth phase. A lethal change cannot found a viable colony, and a strongly growth-impaired lineage may be underrepresented after expansion. Narrow bottlenecks reduce the opportunity for selection to discriminate among lineages, but they do not make reproduction independent of genotype. Lee et al. (2012) describe repeated single-individual bottlenecks and whole-genome sequencing as a way to minimize selective filtering; the inference is conditional on the growth and calling procedure. Lee et al., introductory MA protocol and Materials and Methods.

An original two-type calculation makes the residual filtering explicit. Suppose mutant and reference counts grow deterministically with per-capita rates $g_m$ and $g_r$ for an interval $T$, without further mutation. Define $\Delta g=g_m-g_r$, and let the initial mutant frequency be $x_0\in(0,1)$. The count ratio evolves as

$$\frac{n_m(T)}{n_r(T)}=\frac{x_0}{1-x_0}e^{\Delta gT},\qquad x_T=\frac{x_0e^{\Delta gT}}{1-x_0+x_0e^{\Delta gT}}. \tag{20.2}$$

A uniformly selected cell at the end is mutant with probability $x_T$, rather than $x_0$. Randomness at the final step does not undo differential multiplication before that step. If $\Delta gT$ is small, $x_T-x_0\approx x_0(1-x_0)\Delta gT$; an appreciable disadvantage over a long growth interval can matter even when one cell is eventually chosen. If mutations arise during growth, their birth times matter too, and Equation (20.2) no longer describes their entire history.

Independent lines make the filtering and randomness visible. They are independent experimental units; repeated assays of one line improve measurement precision without replacing biological replication. Extinct or unsequenceable lines must be accounted for rather than silently omitted: conditioning on survival can change both the mutation spectrum and the observed fitness distribution.

A fitness decline is a statistical prediction with assumptions

Lecture 60, 19:37–22:20. The recording motivates a decreasing fitness trajectory when accumulated deleterious effects dominate beneficial effects. A downward trend is a useful expectation under such conditions. It is not a universal law that every line, organism and assay must decline linearly. A line can receive a beneficial change; environments and backgrounds can change an effect; loss of lines can bias the measured survivors. Even a time-linear mean in one fitness coordinate need not be linear in another.

Let the number of retained mutation events along one ideal lineage after $G$ generations be $K\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(UG)$. Here $U$ is a genome-level retained-event rate in the model. Let the log-fitness effects $S_1,S_2,\ldots$ be independent and identically distributed, independent of $K$, with finite first and second moments. Define relative fitness $W$ by

$$Y=\log W=\sum_{j=1}^{K}S_j.$$

Conditioning on $K$ gives $\mathbb E[Y\mid K]=K\mathbb E[S]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid K)=K\operatorname{Var}(S)$. The laws of total expectation and total variance then yield

$$\mathbb E[Y]=UG\,\mathbb E[S],\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y)=UG\,\mathbb E[S^2]. \tag{20.3}$$

The second result includes randomness in the number of events: $UG\operatorname{Var}(S)+UG(\mathbb E[S])^2=UG\mathbb E[S^2]$. It is incorrect to retain only the first term. Negative $\mathbb E[S]$ gives a mean log-fitness decline, while the between-line variance grows with exposure. It does not guarantee a decreasing realization in every line.

Even $\exp(\mathbb E[Y])$ differs from $\mathbb E[W]$. If the required exponential moment exists, conditioning on $K$ and summing the Poisson generating function gives

$$\mathbb E[W]=\exp\!\left(UG\{\mathbb E[e^S]-1\}\right). \tag{20.4}$$

For an identical effect $S=-a$, with $a>0$, the mean log fitness is $-UGa$, its variance is $UGa^2$, and mean fitness is $\exp(UG(e^{-a}-1))$. A short-window Taylor approximation can look linear in $G$; its validity depends on a small accumulated effect, not solely on a small effect per mutation. Epistasis, changing mutation rates and selective loss violate the independent, constant-effect construction. The lecture’s intuition becomes quantitative only after those assumptions and the assay definition have been specified.

A mutation-rate denominator counts lineage exposure

Lecture 60, 22:20–24:59. Sequencing an ancestor and endpoint lines can identify changes retained along those lineages. Base-pair substitutions, short insertions and deletions, and larger changes require different calling procedures and may have different detection probabilities. An endpoint count is not the total number of mutational events among every cell ever produced in all colonies: almost all of those cells leave no sampled descendant.

Consider an ideal constant-rate model for a specified detectable mutation class. Line $i$ has $G_i$ relevant ancestral generations and $L_i$ callable sites, with an independent Poisson count $M_i$ of mean $\mu L_iG_i$. The per-site, per-generation rate is $\mu$. Define $E=\sum_iL_iG_i$ and $M=\sum_iM_i$. Independence gives $M\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu E)$. Up to constants that do not depend on $\mu$, the log likelihood is

$$\ell(\mu)=M\log\mu-\mu E+\text{constant}.$$

For $E>0$ and $M>0$, differentiating gives $M/\mu-E=0$, and the second derivative $-M/\mu^2$ is negative. Thus

$$\widehat{\mu}=\frac{M}{E},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\mu})=\frac{\mu}{E},\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}=\frac{\sqrt M}{E}. \tag{20.5}$$

Exposure can differ across lines; replacing every $L_iG_i$ by an unverified common value biases the denominator. At zero observed mutations the maximum-likelihood estimate is zero, but the underlying rate is not proved zero. Under this same Poisson model a one-sided upper bound with tail probability $\alpha$ solves $\Pr_\mu(M=0)=e^{-\mu E}=\alpha$, giving $\mu_{\mathrm{upper}}=-\log\alpha/E$.

For a synthetic example, 64 lines each with 4,000 generations and four million callable sites produce a combined exposure of $1.024\times10^{12}$ site-generations. If 512 substitutions are called, $\widehat\mu=5\times10^{-10}$ per site-generation. For a genome-level count over the same common callable region, divide by $64\times4{,}000$ instead, yielding $0.002$ changes per lineage-generation. These are two units for this chosen denominator, not interchangeable rate definitions for genomes with different callable regions.

The model excludes recurrent hits that collapse into one endpoint difference, imperfect calling, rate changes during the experiment, line dependence, and selective survival. If line counts are more variable than a common-rate Poisson model permits, the first response is to investigate these assumptions. More replicate lines narrow sampling uncertainty; they do not automatically remove shared systematic errors in generation counts or variant detection.

Why the best competitor need not exclude every other species

Lecture 60, 24:58–28:07. The closing ecological question asks why several species persist together and how communities respond to perturbation. The question cannot be resolved by assigning each species a single environment-independent fitness. Resource depletion, interactions and spatial structure can make the growth rate depend on the current community. A count of isolated species is also conditioned on the cultivation method; it is not automatically a complete census of all organisms in the sample.

As an original mathematical example, take dimensionless densities $n_1,n_2$ and rescaled time, with

$$\dot n_1=n_1(1-n_1-\alpha n_2),\qquad \dot n_2=n_2(1-\beta n_1-n_2). \tag{20.6}$$

Both types have the same growth rate when rare in an otherwise empty environment. Their interaction coefficients differ from their within-type competition coefficients, which have been normalized to one. Species 1 can invade the species-2-only equilibrium $(0,1)$ if $1-\alpha>0$; species 2 can invade $(1,0)$ if $1-\beta>0$. These are local invasion tests, not an explanation of every empirical community.

When $0\leq\alpha,\beta<1$, the positive equilibrium is $n_1^*=(1-\alpha)/(1-\alpha\beta)$ and $n_2^*=(1-\beta)/(1-\alpha\beta)$. At that equilibrium the Jacobian is

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}-n_1^*&-\alpha n_1^*\\-\beta n_2^*&-n_2^*\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its trace is negative and its determinant is $n_1^*n_2^*(1-\alpha\beta)>0$, so both eigenvalues have negative real part. Small deterministic perturbations therefore decay locally. Stability here means that response near this equilibrium, not immunity to large environmental changes, extinction in a finite population, or robustness of every member of a many-species microbiome. An experimentally measured stability question must specify the perturbation and what counts as recovery.

Horizontal transfer changes the ancestry of genetic material

Lecture 60, 28:07–32:34. Vertical inheritance passes genetic material from parent to descendant. Horizontal gene transfer can introduce genetic material from another lineage, through such routes as conjugation, phage-mediated transduction, or uptake of extracellular DNA by a competent cell. Acquisition is not equivalent to stable inheritance: DNA must persist or integrate, and expression and reproductive consequences depend on the recipient and environment. A whole-organism family tree can therefore differ from the history of a transferred gene.

An individual cell can change its genotype by acquisition, whereas evolutionary change in a population still concerns the distribution and transmission of heritable variation. Vertical mutation is not restricted to one-base incremental improvements, either: deletions, duplications and other changes can have large effects. The conceptual distinction is the route by which genetic material is acquired, not a universal ordering of effect sizes.

For an original constant-density model, let $x$ be the fraction of cells carrying a transferable element. Assume mass-action donor–recipient transfer contributes $\gamma x(1-x)$ per unit time, while element loss contributes $\ell x$. Let carriers and noncarriers otherwise have equal vertical reproduction. Then

$$\dot x=x\{\gamma(1-x)-\ell\}. \tag{20.7}$$

For $\gamma>\ell\geq0$, the positive equilibrium is $x^*=1-\ell/\gamma$. Its derivative is $\ell-\gamma<0$, so it is locally stable; an introduced rare element grows at rate $\gamma-\ell$. If $\gamma<\ell$, the element declines. At $\gamma=\ell>0$, decline is quadratic, $\dot x=-\gamma x^2$, rather than exponential. At $x=0$ there are no donors and this closed model cannot create the element. Introducing extracellular or immigrant sources requires an additional term. Selection costs, donor compatibility, density variation and finite-number extinction are excluded here.

This model shows why a transferable element’s persistence cannot be inferred from vertical reproductive fitness alone. It is a mathematical bridge to the lecture’s open question, not a fitted model of a natural plasmid. Transfer, maintenance and host reproduction are separate processes whose rates and evidence must be measured.

Six exercises with full solutions

1. A timing advantage without a sequence change

In the irreversible-window construction, take $k=2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and windows $0.2$ and $1$ second. Derive the early-capture probabilities and the smallest window that gives a probability of at least $0.9$. Explain why none of these results alone establishes a fitness advantage.

Solution

The survival equation is $S'=-2S$, with $S(0)=1$, so $S(t)=e^{-2t}$. The respective capture probabilities are $1-e^{-0.4}=0.329679954$ and $1-e^{-2}=0.864664717$. For the threshold, $1-e^{-2\tau}\geq0.9$ is equivalent to $e^{-2\tau}\leq0.1$, or $\tau\geq\log(10)/2=1.151292546$ seconds. Monotonicity follows from $dp/d\tau=2e^{-2\tau}>0$, so this is the smallest window in the model. Early capture is only a folding-route observable. The cell might need another conformation, compensate for altered yield, or encounter an environment in which the biochemical change has no reproductive consequence. Measurements of final conformation and cell competition are additional inference steps.

2. Uniform sampling after unequal multiplication

Initially $x_0=0.1$. Over a growth interval, the mutant multiplies by half the reference multiplier, so $e^{\Delta gT}=1/2$. Find the mutant probability in a uniformly chosen endpoint cell. Does a one-cell bottleneck eliminate the growth disadvantage?

Solution

The initial odds are $0.1/0.9=1/9$. Multiplying those odds by $1/2$ gives $1/18$. Converting odds $o$ into frequency $o/(1+o)$ yields $x_T=(1/18)/(19/18)=1/19\approx0.0526316$. The chosen cell is mutant with this probability, and reference otherwise. The random bottleneck is unbiased with respect to the endpoint cells; those cells are already the result of unequal multiplication. It therefore does not restore the initial probability $0.1$. Repeating this calculation for a mutation born late in growth requires replacing the full $T$ by the interval after its birth and using its actual initial count. Treating all events as present at the colony’s founding would exaggerate the exposure to selection.

3. Counts, exposure, and a zero-count upper bound

Use the 64-line synthetic example, with 4,000 generations per line, four million callable sites per line, and 512 called substitutions. Compute the per-site rate, genome-level rate over that common region, and their plug-in Poisson standard errors. If the count had been zero, compute the one-sided 95% genome-level upper bound under the same exposure model.

Solution

Total lineage generations are $E_G=64(4{,}000)=256{,}000$. Site-generation exposure is $E=4\times10^6E_G=1.024\times10^{12}$. The estimates are $512/E=5\times10^{-10}$ per site-generation and $512/E_G=0.002$ per lineage-generation over the common callable region. Since $\sqrt{512}=22.627416998$, their standard errors are $2.209708691\times10^{-11}$ and $0.00008838834765$, respectively. These are plug-in sampling standard errors, rather than exact confidence limits or estimates of systematic uncertainty. At zero events, solve $e^{-U E_G}=0.05$, giving $U_{\mathrm{upper}}=2.995732274/256{,}000=0.0000117020792$. The bound is conditional on independent Poisson events and the declared exposure. An uncallable genomic region or an undetected class of variants is not covered by that absence-of-events statement.

4. Mean log fitness and mean fitness answer different questions

Assume $U=0.002$, $G=4{,}000$, and every retained mutation has log effect $S=-0.01$. Calculate the mean and variance of log fitness, $\exp(\mathbb E[\log W])$, and $\mathbb E[W]$. Under the additional identical-deleterious-effect assumption, show how slopes of mean log fitness and log-fitness variance identify $U$ and the effect magnitude $a$.

Solution

The mean event count is $UG=8$. Consequently $\mathbb E[Y]=8(-0.01)=-0.08$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=8(0.01)^2=0.0008$. Exponentiating the mean log fitness gives $e^{-0.08}=0.923116346$. The arithmetic mean fitness is instead $\exp(8(e^{-0.01}-1))\approx0.923484439$. The latter is larger, consistent with Jensen’s inequality for the convex exponential. If $A=-d\mathbb E[Y]/dG=Ua>0$ and $B=d\operatorname{Var}(Y)/dG=Ua^2>0$, division gives $a=B/A$ and $U=A^2/B$. Here $A=0.00002$ and $B=0.0000002$, reproducing $a=0.01$ and $U=0.002$. For a distribution of effects these two slopes identify only $-U\mathbb E[S]$ and $U\mathbb E[S^2]$. They do not uniquely identify a general effect distribution or $U$ without additional assumptions. Fitness-assay noise must also be separated from biological between-line variance before using the moment argument.

5. A coexistence equilibrium and its local relaxation rates

Set $\alpha=1/2$ and $\beta=1/4$ in Equation (20.6). Find the positive equilibrium, test mutual invasion, and calculate the Jacobian’s eigenvalues. State the precise stability conclusion.

Solution

The equilibrium equations are $n_1+n_2/2=1$ and $n_1/4+n_2=1$. Substitution gives $n_2=6/7$ and $n_1=4/7$. Both invasion rates are positive: $1-\alpha=1/2$ and $1-\beta=3/4$. At coexistence, $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-4/7&-2/7\\-3/14&-6/7\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its trace is $-10/7$ and determinant is $3/7$. The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2+(10/7)\lambda+3/7$, whose roots are $-1$ and $-3/7$. Perturbations along the corresponding linear modes decay as $e^{-t}$ and $e^{-3t/7}$ in rescaled time. Thus the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable in this deterministic model. The calculation neither predicts finite-population extinction probabilities nor proves resilience to a perturbation that changes the interaction coefficients or removes a species entirely. At the boundary, a missing species remains absent unless an introduction mechanism is supplied.

6. Transfer can sustain an element without a vertical advantage

Let $\gamma=0.4$ and $\ell=0.1$ per unit time in Equation (20.7), with no vertical reproductive advantage. Find the nonzero equilibrium and its local relaxation rate. Explain what changes at $\gamma=\ell>0$, and whether a donor-free population can acquire the element in this model.

Solution

The right-hand side is $f(x)=x(0.3-0.4x)$, so its roots are $0$ and $0.75$. At the nonzero root, $f'(x)=0.3-0.8x$ gives $f'(0.75)=-0.3$: a small displacement decays at rate $0.3$ per unit time. At zero the derivative is $0.3$, so an introduced rare element grows deterministically. If $\gamma=\ell>0$, $f(x)=-\gamma x^2$ and separation gives $x(t)=x_0/(1+\gamma x_0t)$ for $x_0>0$; decline is algebraic, and linearization has a zero derivative rather than a negative exponential relaxation rate. If $x_0=0$, the right-hand side remains zero. Horizontal transfer here requires a donor already present. Uptake from an external pool or immigration would add a source; stochastic establishment would require a finite-population model. The positive equilibrium does not establish persistence of every individual element lineage.

What to carry into the next experiment

The course began with a question about the origin and persistence of biological variation. The later mathematical chapters made that question precise by specifying birth, death, mutation and sampling events. The experimental chapters then showed that the observable is often further downstream: a competition ratio, an inhibition threshold, a sequence difference, a replay outcome or an archived population. Those measurements answer different questions, and their units and conditioning events are part of the answer.

For a new study, write down the heritable unit, the sampling unit, the relevant clock and the measured phenotype before choosing an equation. Then state how a variant reaches that measurement: production, within-growth filtering, transfer, survival, calling, and assay. Ask which comparison could separate two plausible mechanisms. This practice connects an origin–fixation model to its experimental limits, and it exposes where ecology, molecular kinetics and horizontal inheritance require additional state variables.

A concrete computational extension is to combine the mutation-accumulation construction with a lineage-dependent mutation rate and a noisy fitness assay. Compare inference from endpoint sequence counts with inference from fitness moments, deliberately letting some lines be lost. The point is to determine which parameters remain identifiable under the observation process. Equations that describe the underlying process can still give misleading estimates when the data are generated through a different sampling model.
