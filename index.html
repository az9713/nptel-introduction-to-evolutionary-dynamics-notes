Learning route and prerequisites

This edition follows the 60 numbered recordings of Introduction to Evolutionary Dynamics by Prof. Supreet Saini. Recording numbers determine reading order; the YouTube playlist has a different position order. Twenty topic chapters keep related arguments together while preserving lecture order. The introduction supplies orientation.

Begin with evolutionary reasoning and molecular foundations, then learn growth and competition models. Build mutation-effect and fitness-landscape concepts before stochastic establishment and fixation. Finally connect the models to experimental evolution, innovation, diversification, and mutation accumulation.

Helpful preparation includes elementary molecular biology, logarithms and exponentials, single-variable calculus, elementary probability, and Python. Mathematical sections must define their additional assumptions and notation as they arise. Biological observations, formal models, and original teaching examples are distinguished in each chapter's source map.

ChaptersCentral questionWhat to carry forward
1–4What changes, and how is biological information transmitted?Observation versus mechanism; genotype, phenotype, replication and mutation.
5–8What does fitness mean in a specified environment?Growth and competition, assay dependence, mutation effects and landscape structure.
9–11Which lineages survive finite-population chance?Sampling laws, neutral symmetry, birth/death rules, selected fixation and explicit clocks.
12–14How do concurrent mutations and sequence comparisons change inference?Waiting versus sweeping, traveling distributions, opportunity normalization and sampling timescale.
15–20Which experiments distinguish competing explanations?Culture design, long-term trajectories, contingency, diversification and direct mutation-effect measurements.

Use the numerical lab alongside chapters 5–6 and 9–11. Change one assumption at a time: population size, relative reproductive weight, event versus generation clock, or growth-rate difference. Before comparing a graph with an experiment, identify its observable, units, sampling process and omitted mechanism.
