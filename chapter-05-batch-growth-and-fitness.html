Batch growth and what fitness measures

Lectures 14–17 · Finite resources, growth phases, and competition

A resident strain A begins dividing promptly after transfer into fresh medium. A mutant strain B waits longer but then divides faster. Which strain is fitter? A growth-rate comparison favours B; an early census can favour A. This is a consequential ambiguity: repeated transfers select for performance across the entire growth cycle, while a measurement confined to exponential growth selects a different trait. We will construct the assay before assigning a fitness number.

Our running example is an original teaching construction, not a reconstruction of measured Escherichia coli data. Both strains begin with 100 cells. A has a one-hour lag and an exponential growth rate of 0.6 per hour; B has a three-hour lag and a rate of 0.8 per hour. First we hold the observation time fixed and ignore resource exhaustion. Then we impose a finite shared capacity and ask which conclusions survive. The distinction between these two experiments will matter as much as the difference between the strains.

1. Constructing a batch-culture measurement Lecture 14, 11:19

A batch culture begins with a finite amount of growth medium and an inoculum, the cells used to seed it. During the experiment there is no continuous nutrient feed. Mixing and temperature control can reduce some environmental variation, but nutrient concentrations, waste products, and population density change as growth proceeds. A closed nutrient budget does not imply a perfectly closed physical system: an aerated flask can exchange gases with its surroundings.

The lectures motivate microbial experiments through short generation times, controllable conditions, DNA sequencing, and the possibility of preserving samples. A frozen sample can provide a historical population for later comparison with descendants. This is an experimental archive with viability and storage limits, not a claim that every microbe can be stored indefinitely. When ancestral and evolved samples are revived together, the revival protocol itself becomes part of the comparison: physiological history can alter the first growth cycle even without a DNA-sequence difference.

Let \(X(t)\) be the number of cells in a fixed culture volume at elapsed time \(t\), and let \(C(t)=X(t)/V\) be their concentration when that volume is \(V\). A growth curve is a sequence of estimates of one of these quantities. Its interpretation depends on what the instrument actually observes. Two common measurements answer related but distinct questions.

Optical density is a logarithmic attenuation measurement. For a blank-reference intensity \(I_0\) and transmitted intensity \(I\), define \(\mathrm{OD}=\log_{10}(I_0/I)\). In a microbial suspension, scattering contributes to the attenuation. OD is therefore a proxy whose conversion to cell concentration requires calibration under the relevant instrument, geometry, medium, and cell morphology. It does not identify living cells individually.

A colony-forming unit, or CFU, is an entity that produces a visible colony under the chosen plating conditions. If a fraction \(d\) of the original concentration is retained after dilution, a volume \(v\) millilitres is plated, and \(n\) colonies grow, the estimated concentration is

\[ \widehat C_{\mathrm{CFU}}=\frac{n}{vd} \quad\text{CFU per millilitre}. \tag{5.1} \]

The denominator is the equivalent volume of undiluted culture. One colony need not mean one original cell: clumps can produce a single colony, and some viable cells fail to grow in the selected medium or incubation period. CFU measures reproductive recovery in that protocol. Microscopic counts, CFU counts, and OD can diverge without any measurement being arithmetically wrong.

This distinction has direct experimental support. Stevenson and colleagues studied OD calibration in bacterial and yeast cultures and found that changing cell geometry changes the relation between OD and directly counted concentration. Their Results subsection “Calibration of OD typically changes with growth conditions and cell size” and Methods specify the organisms, growth conditions, and counting procedure. The consequence for our assay is limited but important: an uncalibrated OD difference cannot by itself establish a difference in cell number or reproductive fitness. Stevenson et al., 2016.

2. Growth phases are different components of performance Lecture 15, 0:17

The familiar batch curve contains a lag phase, an interval of adaptation with little net population increase; a log phase, approximately exponential growth; a stationary phase, approximately constant population size or measured signal; and sometimes a death phase, declining recoverable abundance. These are descriptions of a curve, not four universal states with sharp boundaries. A mixed population can contain actively dividing, nondividing, dying, and metabolically different cells at the same time.

The lecture uses lactose metabolism to explain why the initial interval can depend on prior conditions. Lactose utilisation requires transport and metabolic machinery: LacY is a transporter, and the LacZ enzyme, β-galactosidase, hydrolyses lactose. A cell transferred from another carbon source may need to adjust expression and physiology before rapid growth. The point is not that every required protein is literally absent before transfer. The point is that a fixed genome can produce different transient growth curves after different histories.

Accordingly, lag duration is an assay-dependent phenotype, a measurable characteristic arising from genotype and condition. It can change through a mutation, through the culture’s previous medium, or through how the investigator chooses the onset criterion. Exponential rate, final yield, survival in stationary conditions, and recovery after starvation are additional characteristics. None is automatically interchangeable with the others.

A plateau also requires care. Constancy of a population count establishes that its net change is near zero, not that all births and deaths have stopped. Turnover or recycling can occur beneath a nearly constant count. Conversely, a constant optical signal need not establish a constant viable population. A death-phase decline is especially clear in a CFU assay because it records loss of colony-forming ability under its recovery conditions.

For the running comparison, define the lag times \(\lambda_A,\lambda_B\), in hours, and the post-lag per-capita growth rates \(g_A,g_B\), in inverse hours. A deliberately simple curve holds each strain constant during lag and gives it exponential growth afterward:

\[ A(t)=A_0e^{g_A(t-\lambda_A)_+},\qquad B(t)=B_0e^{g_B(t-\lambda_B)_+},\qquad y_+=\max(y,0). \tag{5.2} \]

The corner at the end of lag is a modelling approximation. The model excludes resource depletion and death, so it is useful only for a prescribed interval in which those omissions are tolerable. It makes the competition between a short lag and a fast subsequent rate transparent.

3. Exponential growth and the doubling-time conversion Lecture 16, 8:05

Suppose that the expected net increase per cell per unit time is the constant \(g\). In a deterministic large-population approximation,

\[ \frac{dX}{dt}=gX,\qquad X(0)=X_0>0. \tag{5.3} \]

The derivative is the limit of an average change per time interval, not a finite change over an arbitrarily selected interval. The proportionality assumption is the biological hinge: each additional cell contributes the same net rate while the environment is sufficiently unchanged. Birth minus death can give a net \(g\); a positive value need not mean that no deaths occur.

Divide by the positive population and integrate from 0 to \(t\). The ratio inside the logarithm avoids taking a logarithm of a dimensional count:

\[ \int_{X_0}^{X(t)}\frac{du}{u}=\int_0^t g\,d\tau, \qquad \ln\frac{X(t)}{X_0}=gt, \qquad X(t)=X_0e^{gt}. \tag{5.4} \]

Thus a plot of \(\ln[X(t)/X_0]\) against time has slope \(g\) over an exponential interval. A base-10 logarithmic plot has slope \(g/\ln 10\), so the numerical slope must be converted before it is called a per-capita rate. Counts should also be blank-corrected and calibrated when an optical proxy is substituted.

The doubling time \(t_d\) is the time required to multiply the population by two under the constant-rate model. Equation (5.4) gives \(e^{gt_d}=2\), hence

\[ t_d=\frac{\ln 2}{g},\qquad g=\frac{\ln 2}{t_d}. \tag{5.5} \]

A 20-minute doubling time corresponds to \(g=0.0346574\) per minute, or (2.07944) per hour. The recording’s reciprocal-time shorthand requires this logarithmic conversion: (1/20) per minute would give a 20-minute multiplication by \(e\), not by two. Over 72 hours, a 20-minute binary doubling schedule contains 216 doublings and predicts \(X_0 2^{216}\), approximately \(1.05\times10^{65}X_0\). Its absurd extrapolation is a failure of the constant environment assumption, not of integration.

The lecture’s geometric cell-volume estimate makes this failure tangible. A cylinder 2 micrometres long and 1 micrometre in diameter has volume \(\pi/2\) cubic micrometres. At a water-like density of \(10^3\) kilograms per cubic metre, that idealised cell has mass \((\pi/2)10^{-15}\) kilograms. Combining that fixed mass with 216 unlimited doublings produces about \(1.65\times10^{50}\) kilograms from one initial cell. Neither a flask nor its substrate budget supplies that mass. The calculation is an illustrative geometric estimate; cell size and composition need not remain fixed during actual growth.

4. Logistic growth: a finite capacity and its exact consequences Lecture 17, 7:35

A logistic model replaces the constant per-capita rate by a rate declining linearly with population. Let \(K>0\) be the carrying capacity, the equilibrium count in this model under the specified conditions. For \(g>0\),

\[ \frac{dX}{dt}=gX\left(1-\frac{X}{K}\right). \tag{5.6} \]

Both \(X\) and \(K\) are counts in the same fixed volume; concentration versions require both to be concentrations. The factor \(1-X/K\) is dimensionless. Capacity summarises environmental limitations rather than representing a universal property of a strain: medium, temperature, aeration, and growth protocol can change the observed saturation level. A nonpermissive environment cannot be represented by inserting \(K=0\) into (5.6), because the equation is then undefined.

For \(0<X_0<K\), separation of variables uses \(K/[X(K-X)]=1/X+1/(K-X)\). Integrating gives

\[ \ln\frac{X(t)}{K-X(t)}- \ln\frac{X_0}{K-X_0}=gt, \qquad X(t)=\frac{K}{1+(K/X_0-1)e^{-gt}}. \tag{5.7} \]

The first identity is linear growth of the log odds of occupancy \(X/K\). It proves more than the sketch of a sigmoid: \(X(t)\) increases, remains below \(K\) at every finite time, and approaches \(K\) asymptotically. Starting exactly at \(K\) gives a constant solution. Starting above \(K\) gives decline toward \(K\), which is a mathematical net-loss prediction of this model rather than an explanation of how cells die.

The exponential approximation is controlled by density. At a given count, the relative reduction in per-capita rate from \(g\) is \(X/K\). To keep that instantaneous reduction below 1%, require \(X/K<0.01\). “Early” is useful only insofar as the initial density is low enough. At \(X=K/2\), the instantaneous per-capita rate is \(g/2\); it does not remain \(g/2\), since the count immediately changes. Treating the subsequent curve as an exponential with constant rate \(g/2\) silently changes the equation.

The absolute growth rate is \(gX-gX^2/K\), a concave quadratic in \(X\). Differentiating it with respect to \(X\) gives \(g(1-2X/K)\), so the largest count increase per unit time occurs at \(X=K/2\) and equals \(gK/4\). This is also the curve’s inflection point. A large per-capita rate and a large absolute increase are different concepts: near zero the per-capita rate is nearly \(g\), but there are few cells contributing to the total increase.

For \(X_0=100\), \(K=10{,}000\), and \(g=0.6\) per hour, the half-capacity time is \(\ln 99/0.6=7.65853\) hours. Logistic growth alone has no physiological lag and, from an initial count below capacity, no death phase. Fitting its three parameters to a four-phase curve therefore requires an explicit choice of fitting interval; a good fit on that interval is not evidence that the omitted phases are absent.

5. Two strains compete for one capacity Lecture 17, 27:24

The lecture’s extension uses a common crowding factor, because A and B consume the same limiting resources. With no lag in this second model and positive initial counts,

\[ \dot A=g_A A q,\qquad \dot B=g_B B q,\qquad q=1-\frac{A+B}{K},\qquad A_0+B_0<K. \tag{5.8} \]

Giving each strain an independent \(K\) would allow a combined equilibrium of \(2K\) and would model separate resources. Equation (5.8) instead permits a continuum of equilibria \(A+B=K\). The genotype determines its low-density growth rate; the shared environment determines how much of that rate is realised.

Define the mutant frequency \(x=B/(A+B)\). Quotient differentiation, with the total population’s derivative included, yields

\[ \dot x=(g_B-g_A)x(1-x)q,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\ln\frac{B}{A}=(g_B-g_A)q. \tag{5.9} \]

When \(q>0\), the faster-growing strain increases in frequency. When resources are exhausted in this model, \(q=0\) and selection through growth ceases. The fact that B increases does not establish that it reaches frequency one. The available growth opportunity is finite.

This can be proved without a numerical integration. Introduce cumulative growth exposure \(z(t)=\int_0^t q(\tau)\,d\tau\), measured in hours. Then \(A=A_0e^{g_Az}\), \(B=B_0e^{g_Bz}\). The increasing function \(F(z)=A_0e^{g_Az}+B_0e^{g_Bz}\) begins below \(K\) and eventually exceeds it, so it has a unique positive root \(F(z_*)=K\). Since \(\dot z=1-F(z)/K\), exposure increases toward \(z_*\) and cannot cross it. Therefore

\[ A_*=A_0e^{g_Az_*}>0,\qquad B_*=B_0e^{g_Bz_*}>0,\qquad \ln\frac{B_*}{B_0}=\frac{g_B}{g_A}\ln\frac{A_*}{A_0}. \tag{5.10} \]

In our shared-capacity construction, \(A_0=B_0=100\), \(g_A=0.6\), \(g_B=0.8\), and \(K=10{,}000\). The limiting exposure is 5.390394 hours, giving \(A_*=2538.698\), \(B_*=7461.302\), and \(x_*=0.746130\). These are limiting counts; no finite stopping time attains them exactly. B gains a substantial fraction but A persists. This frozen composition is not ecological evidence for stable coexistence: renewed resources restart differential growth.

6. Fitness belongs to an assay Lecture 15, 24:31

Return to the lagged construction. Once both strains have exited lag, their relative log increase by a fixed endpoint \(T\) is

\[ \ell(T)=\ln\frac{B(T)/B_0}{A(T)/A_0} =(g_B-g_A)T-g_B\lambda_B+g_A\lambda_A. \tag{5.11} \]

Here \(\ell\) is a dimensionless log-fold advantage for one assay interval. Define the corresponding relative fold multiplication \(r_{\mathrm{cycle}}=e^{\ell}\); if desired, its dimensionless selection coefficient is \(s_{\mathrm{cycle}}=r_{\mathrm{cycle}}-1\). Neither is an absolute rate. The rate difference \(\Delta g=g_B-g_A\) has units of inverse hours; the growth-rate ratio \(g_B/g_A\) is dimensionless but is yet another quantity. A numerical comparison is meaningful only after choosing which of these objects is measured.

For the chosen lags and rates, \(\ell(T)=0.2T-1.8\) for \(T\ge3\) hours. The endpoint at which the advantage changes sign is 9 hours. At 4 hours B has frequency 0.268941 despite its faster subsequent growth. At 12 hours its frequency is 0.645656. These unlimited-resource values cannot be used after a finite flask has saturated; they isolate the lag-rate trade-off rather than predicting an actual long batch curve.

For any positive initial frequency \(x_0\), the definition of log-fold advantage gives the exact odds update

\[ \frac{x_T}{1-x_T}=e^\ell\frac{x_0}{1-x_0}, \qquad x_T=\frac{x_0e^\ell}{1-x_0+x_0e^\ell}. \tag{5.12} \]

A common dilution of A and B preserves this frequency in a deterministic approximation. A transfer that samples a finite number of cells adds sampling variation, and a genotype-dependent recovery probability adds another selection component. Those effects are outside (5.12) unless incorporated into the measured fold multiplications.

There is a useful research extension beyond our fixed endpoint. Manhart, Adkar, and Shakhnovich model competition through lag, growth, and resource-limited saturation; in their model the exhaustion time depends on the competing strains’ frequencies and yields. Their Methods equation (2.2) calls the log-fold advantage \(s\); we call it \(\ell\) to distinguish it from \(r-1\). Their equation (2.3) is the same odds update as (5.12). The dependence of the stopping time on composition can make selection frequency dependent, even when the strains interact only through one resource. This is a model result under stated assumptions, not a universal empirical law. Manhart et al., 2018, §§2–3.

7. Executable comparison and independent checks

The standard-library companion chapter05_batch.py evaluates (5.2) and solves the monotone exposure equation in (5.10) by bisection. Inputs are synthetic counts, rates in inverse hours, times in hours, and a capacity in counts. It neither fits data nor simulates mutation. Invalid nonpositive counts, rates, or an initially exceeded capacity are rejected by the shared-capacity routine. Extremely large exponential arguments can overflow; the example keeps them modest.

Run the following listing beside the companion file. The assertions check both the total resource limit and the exact logarithmic invariant. In addition, a separate validation integrated the two original differential equations with fourth-order Runge–Kutta and recovered these limiting counts, so the numerical check does not merely repeat the bisection algorithm.

from math import isclose, log
from chapter05_batch import lagged_count, limiting_composition

for time in (4.0, 9.0, 12.0):
    a = lagged_count(100.0, 0.6, 1.0, time)
    b = lagged_count(100.0, 0.8, 3.0, time)
    print(f"t={time:4.1f} h: A={a:9.3f}, B={b:9.3f}, "
          f"x={b / (a + b):.6f}")
z, a, b, x = limiting_composition(100, 100, 0.6, 0.8, 10000)
assert isclose(a + b, 10000, rel_tol=1e-12)
assert isclose(log(b / 100), (0.8 / 0.6) * log(a / 100))
print(f"shared limit: z={z:.6f} h, A={a:.3f}, B={b:.3f}, "
      f"x={x:.6f}")

8. Worked exercises

Exercise 1. What was counted?

A 0.1-millilitre plate from a \(10^{-6}\) dilution yields 150 colonies. Estimate CFU per millilitre. Under an independent Poisson counting approximation, estimate its relative sampling uncertainty. Explain why an OD-based cell count could disagree.

Solution

The equivalent original volume is \(10^{-7}\) millilitres, so the estimate is \(150/10^{-7}=1.5\times10^9\) CFU per millilitre. A Poisson count of mean near 150 has standard deviation about \(\sqrt{150}\); scaling to concentration retains relative standard error \(1/\sqrt{150}=0.08165\), about 8.2%. This approximation describes counting variation, not dilution errors or plating bias. OD responds to scattering, while CFU responds to colony formation; changed cell size, clumping, and differential recovery can make their estimates diverge. The discrepancy needs an assay explanation, not automatic attribution to a different growth rate.

Exercise 2. Two meanings of “rate”

A report states “one division every 20 minutes” and uses \(g=0.05\) per minute in an exponential model. Check the conversion, and compare the predicted multiplication over an hour with the binary doubling schedule.

Solution

Binary doublings give three doublings per hour and a factor of eight. The correct exponential parameter is \(\ln2/20=0.0346574\) per minute, yielding \(e^{60g}=8\). The proposed value gives \(e^3=20.0855\), corresponding to a doubling time \(\ln2/0.05=13.8629\) minutes. The reciprocal doubling time counts doubling intervals per minute; it becomes a natural-log growth rate only after multiplication by \(\ln2\). A synchronous binary schedule and a smooth exponential curve differ between division events, but they agree at the specified doubling endpoints when parameterised consistently.

Exercise 3. A lag-rate crossover

For our lagged strains, derive the endpoint at which their counts are equal. Then suppose B starts at frequency 0.01. Does the sign-changing endpoint for relative advantage change? At 12 hours, calculate B’s frequency.

Solution

Equal initial counts give equality when \(\ell(T)=0.2T-1.8=0\), so \(T=9\) hours, which lies after both lags. With a different initial frequency, \(\ell\) still compares fold increases and its zero remains 9 hours. Equality of final counts is a different question: it additionally requires \(\ell=\ln[(1-x_0)/x_0]\). At 12 hours, \(\ell=0.6\), and (5.12) gives \(x_T=0.01e^{0.6}/(0.99+0.01e^{0.6})=0.0180726\). B has a favourable fold advantage but remains uncommon. Advantage is neither equality of abundance nor fixation.

Exercise 4. When does exponential growth cease to be accurate?

With \(X_0=100\), \(K=10{,}000\), and \(g=0.6\) per hour, find the time at which the instantaneous per-capita rate has fallen by 10%. Find the maximal absolute increase and explain why it occurs later.

Solution

A 10% rate reduction means \(X/K=0.1\), so \(X=1000\). Rearranging (5.7) gives \(t=g^{-1}\ln[X(K-X_0)/(X_0(K-X))]\); the threshold time is \(\ln11/0.6=3.99649\) hours. The largest absolute increase occurs at \(X=5000\), at time \(\ln99/0.6=7.65853\) hours, and is \(gK/4=1500\) cells per hour. Between these counts, the contribution from a growing number of cells outweighs the decreasing per-cell rate. A local rate error threshold is not the same as a cumulative error bound for an exponential trajectory.

Exercise 5. Faster growth without extinction

In the shared-capacity model with equal initial counts and \(g_B>g_A>0\), prove that B’s frequency increases but remains below one. What changes if the rates are equal? What does a fresh nutrient transfer do?

Solution

Below capacity, \(q>0\), and (5.9) makes \(\dot x>0\) for an interior frequency. The unique finite exposure root exists because \(F(0)<K\), \(F'(z)>0\), and \(F(z)\) diverges. Consequently \(A_*=A_0e^{g_Az_*}\) is positive and \(x_*<1\). If the rates are equal, \(\dot x=0\), so the initial fraction is preserved throughout growth. A common dilution into fresh medium lowers the total count while preserving its expected composition and restores \(q>0\). Differential growth then resumes. Repeated cycles may drive the fraction close to one in a deterministic model, while a finite-cell transfer also permits stochastic loss. The saturated equilibrium is therefore not proof of an attracting coexistence fraction under renewed resources.

Exercise 6. Design a defensible fitness comparison

A mutant has a shorter lag in separate flasks, the resident has a faster exponential slope, and both reach the same final OD. Design a competition assay that can determine which has the larger cycle advantage. Identify at least two controls that change the inference.

Solution

Mix distinguishable A and B at a recorded initial frequency and total concentration, use the same specified medium, temperature, mixing, and transfer history, and measure strain-resolved initial and final abundance. Declare the endpoint, such as a fixed duration or a resource-exhaustion criterion. Compute \(\ell=\ln[B(T)/B_0]-\ln[A(T)/A_0]\), with uncertainty from biological replicate cultures and counting. Equal final OD in separate flasks does not establish equal yield or equal success in mixed culture. Standardise pre-culture conditions to separate heritable differences from history-dependent lag. Swap neutral labels, or verify their effects independently, to detect a marker cost. Repeat at different initial frequencies to test frequency dependence; repeat at different endpoint rules or transfer schedules to expose the lag-growth trade-off. These are different defined assays, so a changed winner can be a coherent biological result rather than a contradiction.
